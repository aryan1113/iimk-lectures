# Sessions 1-3: Why Behavioural Strategy? Flattery, Decision-Making and Nudge

These first sessions set up the whole course. The central claim is simple: strategy is not made by perfectly rational machines but by people, and people are biased, flattered, overconfident and influenced by how choices are presented. If that is true, the place where behavioural insight pays off most is the way managers make decisions. The sessions open with flattery as a warm-up, move to the Mattel case as a story of well-meaning managers going wrong, and end with nudge as a first practical tool.

## Flattery: simple, pervasive and irresistible

The instructor starts with everyday lines you have probably heard: a salesperson saying "you really know your products, I am glad I came here"; "congratulations, you have made a great choice" after a purchase; a consultant telling a CEO "your ability to see the big picture is exceptional"; a boss telling a subordinate "you are probably the most organized person on the team". Compliments and reassurances like these are all forms of flattery.

The slides make three points about why flattery works. First, we love it even when we are not deceived by it. It feeds our self-image, gives us the feeling of being rewarded, and raises our sense of social standing. Second, people find it hard not to like those who think highly of them, so flattery quietly buys goodwill. Third, a compliment creates a social obligation: the person complimented feels pushed to return the favour. The slides also name the **feel-good effect**, which means flattery plays down our negatives and plays up our positives. The line to remember is that knowing the trick does not break the spell. Even a person who sees exactly what the flatterer is doing still feels good.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Think of a shopkeeper who says "you have excellent taste" just before you buy. You may roll your eyes inwardly, and still feel slightly better about the purchase and slightly friendlier toward the shopkeeper. That is the "knowing doesn't help" point in action.

## The costs of flattery

If flattery feels good, why worry? The slides split the costs across three parties.

- **The recipient** gets a distorted self-perception, becomes overconfident, and receives less honest feedback because people stop telling them the truth.
- **The flatterer** pays in credibility and authenticity, and ends up maintaining a false persona.
- **The organization or system** suffers poorer choices, information asymmetry (the boss does not hear what is really going on) and a distorted meritocracy, where people rise by pleasing rather than performing.

An AI-generated line on the slide sums it up: flattery is "social high-fructose corn syrup", an immediate, cheap energy boost that leaves everyone malnourished and worse off in the long run. The slide then adds a wry counter-line, "in the long run we are all dead". (My reading, not stated on the slide: this hints at why people keep flattering, since the benefit is immediate and the cost is delayed.)

The same problem shows up in AI. The slides cite research and the GPT-4o episode on sycophancy: AI systems tell you what you want to hear, not what you need to hear. Sycophantic AI reduces prosocial intentions and promotes dependence. The lesson is that flattery is a design problem for systems as well as a social habit for people.

## Behavioural insights and organizations

The deck then widens the lens. Organizations increasingly use behavioural insights in ever-evolving business models, malls and shopping centres, public policy design, understanding how people decide, and social upliftment and charity. The message is that this is a practical, spreading field, not a classroom curiosity.

## What is strategy, and where does behaviour matter?

The slides pose "What is strategy?" and then show the **strategy process** as a diagram. Industry analysis and analysis of resources and capabilities feed into four steps in sequence: identify the objectives or mission, diagnose performance, formulate strategy, and implement strategy. The question asked is where the impact of a behavioural approach would be felt, and the answer is **decision-making**, which runs through every step of that process. Every box involves someone choosing what to look at, what to conclude and what to do.

## Mattel: where do well-intended managers go astray?

> **In class:** The Mattel case (1998) is the running example for decision-making errors.

Mattel is a toy and entertainment company known for dolls, toys and games, and one of the largest toy companies. In 1998 its revenues were dropping, and the dot-com boom was going on. Mattel acquired The Learning Company, a maker of educational software and games. It later sold the business to Gores Technology Group at a total loss of about $3.6 billion, and the deal is called one of the worst acquisitions of all time. The slide's diagnosis is that top management was overconfident about transferring its skills: "software gaming is different from physical gaming". Being good at making and selling physical toys did not mean Mattel could run a software business.

The deck then asks "right intentions?" The slide only asks the question; it does not state the managers' intent. What the slides do show is falling revenue and the dot-com boom around the deal. A reasonable reading (outside the slides) is that good intentions do not protect against bad decisions. A contrasting slide notes that Mattel later had a windfall from a Hollywood film (Barbie), a reminder that the same company can also get things right. CEO Jill Barad rose from product manager in 1981 to CEO and Chairman in 1997, and sales grew from $200m to $2b during her tenure, and she still stepped down. The question the slides leave you with is: where and how do well-intended managers risk going astray in the decision-making process?

## Where decision-making goes astray

The deck answers with a map of the decision process in three stages.

**Setting an agenda** covers what goes in and out, and who decides; problems over opportunities (noticing opportunities); information corridors; absorptive capacity; and differing processes, systematic versus exceptions. The slides list these as bullets only. Glosses (not from the slides): *information corridors* are the channels through which information reaches decision-makers; *absorptive capacity* is the ability to recognise and use new outside information; handling things systematically versus as exceptions means routine procedure versus case-by-case treatment.

**Representing problems** covers focus areas and framing the problem. (Gloss, not from the slides: the same situation framed as a threat or as an opportunity can lead to different actions.)

**Discovering and choosing alternatives** covers path dependency and working around constraints. (Gloss: path dependency means past choices narrow today's options.)

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Apply this to Mattel. Agenda: falling revenue pushes "diversify into software" onto the table while "improve the core" may drop out. Representing: the problem is framed as "we are missing the digital boom". Alternatives: an acquisition is chosen because it is the visible option. This mapping is an illustration, not something the slides state.

## The significance of the individual

The slides show an "average strategist" through a cartoon character, Suppandi, who stands for the ordinary, unremarkable decision-maker. Against him sit three well-known figures, Rockefeller, Vanderbilt and Dhirubhai Ambani, shown under the heading "the significance of the individual". Today, Bhavish Aggarwal follows Elon Musk in asking employees for weekly accomplishment reports. The slides give no explanation. My reading (outside the slides) is that individuals and their habits shape how big organizations behave.

## The behavioural approach to strategy

The behavioural approach integrates findings from psychology and the relevant parts of sociology. It gives a holistic and **microfoundational** view, meaning it explains firm-level outcomes by starting from the behaviour of the people inside. It is presented as the next frontier of the strategy field, with more explanatory power than conventional strategy, and it is based on evidence. The course is meant for strategy aspirants, aspiring managers and professionals, consultants and problem-solvers, and anyone who joins a firm or helps organize around people.

## Nudge: a first application

A **nudge**, popularized by Thaler and Sunstein, is a light touch or push that influences the behaviour or decisions of individuals or groups. It works on the **choice architecture**, which means the way options are laid out and presented, using positive reinforcement and indirect suggestions. It is meant to be easy and cheap and to leave freedom of choice intact. The slide's examples include putting fruit at eye level. The slide also lists banning junk food, which sits oddly because a ban does remove choice; treat the fruit placement as the clean example.

The slides show a 1938 Austrian referendum (the annexation of Austria into Germany) with no explanation; it presumably illustrates how the presentation of a vote can shape its outcome, but that is an inference.

Nudges are everywhere: phone screen-time alerts, the beep of a smartwatch telling you to move, reusing the hotel towel, warnings before phone calls, reminder emails, and apps. Media examples cited include nudges that raised COVID-19 vaccinations and a government scheme nudging the auto industry toward green vehicles, along with concern about governments using sensitive data to nudge.

> **In class:** Exercise: pick an organizational problem (from work, an internship or the campus) and a societal problem. Ask whether the stakeholders can be nudged toward better choices and design the nudge. The slides give no model answer; the exercise simply asks you to design the nudge.

The session closes with **stickK** as a case for analysis. The deck gives only the question "what is stickK?".

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** stickK is a commitment-contract service in which you set a goal and put something at stake if you fail. It is a natural example of a nudge that uses your own self-control problem. The slides do not discuss its details.

The final slide introduces **prospect theory** as a title only, to be taught next.

## Putting it together

One way to connect these sessions (my synthesis): people like flattery and can become overconfident, managers are people too (Mattel), the decision process has several points where things go wrong (agenda, representation, alternatives), and the behavioural approach gives both a diagnosis and tools such as nudge. A link of my own (not stated on the slides): flattery and Mattel can both be read as cases of biased judgement distorting decisions.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Why do people fall for flattery even when they see through it, and what does it cost?** Outline: it feeds self-image, reward and social standing; creates obligation; knowing the trick does not stop the feel-good effect. Costs fall on the recipient (overconfidence, less feedback), the flatterer (credibility) and the organization (poor choices, distorted meritocracy). Mention sycophantic AI.
2. **Use Mattel to explain where well-intended managers go astray.** Outline: facts of The Learning Company deal; overconfidence in transferring skills; link to agenda setting, problem representation and choosing alternatives.
3. **Where in the strategy process does behavioural insight matter most?** Outline: describe the process; answer decision-making at every step; explain the microfoundational, evidence-based approach.
4. **Define nudge and choice architecture, and design a nudge for a problem you choose.** Outline: definition (light push, no loss of freedom, cheap); state the problem, the stakeholders and the changed choice setting; note why it works.
5. **Why is the behavioural approach claimed to have more explanatory power than conventional strategy?** Outline: it integrates psychology and sociology; builds from individual behaviour up; evidence-based.
