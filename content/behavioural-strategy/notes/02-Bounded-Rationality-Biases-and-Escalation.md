# Sessions 4-6: Bounded Rationality, Bounded Awareness and Escalation of Commitment

Having argued that managers are human, this block explains how human thinking falls short and why that matters for strategy. It starts with the limits of rationality and awareness, moves to escalation of commitment (the bias behind many corporate downfalls), then to the two systems of thinking, and finishes with practical ways to reduce bias. The aim is for you to read a business vignette and say which bias is at work and what to do about it.

## Bounded rationality and satisficing

**Bounded rationality** means decision-making is constrained by information, time and cognitive limits. A manager never has all the facts, never has unlimited time and cannot process everything. The slide title reads "Bounded rationality VS." with nothing after it, so the contrast is left open. **Satisficing** follows directly: instead of searching for the best option, you pick a solution that clears the minimum acceptable threshold. The slide's image is memorable: should we look for the sharpest needle in the haystack, or one sharp enough to sew? (Plain-English gloss, not from the slides: searching for the perfect option can cost more than it is worth, so people stop at "good enough".)

## Bounded awareness: failing to use available information

Bounded awareness is, per the slide, a specific failure to use information that is actually available. The deck shows videos for the first two types.

**Inattentional blindness.** Information is available but escapes awareness because attention is consumed by another demanding task. Slide example: in the race to win market leadership, a company missed the growth in a substitute market. 

**Change blindness.** A failure to notice changes in available information. Example: top management never saw the decline in employee morale while integrating a newly acquired company. 

**Focalism.** Focusing so much on one event or factor that you overestimate how significant it is. Example: a team obsessed with cutting costs missed the aspirational value of owning a car. Kahneman's line sums it up: "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it."

A memory aid (my gloss, not from the slides): inattentional blindness is missing something because of competing attention; change blindness is missing something because it changed; focalism is overweighting the thing you are staring at.

> **In class:** The Vandals game. The class played a game and was then asked which type of bounded awareness it showed, with the comment that there is a fine line between the types. The slides do not record what happened in the game. The lesson on the slide is that people generally overestimate their own awareness and underestimate its bounds.

## Escalation of commitment

Escalation of commitment is continuing to commit to a failing course of action (this wording is the standard definition, outside the slides; the slides give only the name). The slides call it "a special kind of bias" and point out why it matters for strategy: strategic decisions are long-term, cross-functional, path-dependent and irreversible. Because of these traits, escalation explains most downfalls of dominant firms. The slides then ask a fair challenge: can one person's bias really matter so much in a big firm?

The answer is that it is not one bias but a **reinforcement of many**. The slides list factors in four groups.

- **Individual and psychological:** loss aversion, confirmatory bias, locus of control, gambler's fallacy, personal identification with the project, and managing impressions.
- **Social:** pluralistic ignorance, cultural values, group emotions, inter-departmental politics and institutionalized processes.
- **Project:** size, prior experience and salvage costs.
- **External environment:** government policies, stakeholder pressures and competition.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The slides only list these factors by name. The plain-English glosses below are standard meanings, not slide content, and how each applies to escalation is my reading.
>
> - *Loss aversion*: losses hurt more than equal gains please, so people resist admitting a loss.
> - *Confirmatory bias*: favouring evidence that confirms what you already believe.
> - *Locus of control*: whether you believe outcomes are in your own hands or driven by outside forces; a strong belief in control can keep people pushing on.
> - *Gambler's fallacy*: believing a run of bad luck means a win is "due".
> - *Personal identification*: the project becomes part of your own identity, so abandoning it feels personal.
> - *Managing impressions*: not wanting to look as if you made a mistake.
> - *Pluralistic ignorance*: everyone privately doubts the plan but assumes everyone else supports it, so nobody speaks up.
> - *Cultural values*: norms about persistence, face or failure.
> - *Group emotions*: shared feelings (pride, fear) that sway the group.
> - *Inter-departmental politics*: departments protect their own interests.
> - *Institutionalized processes*: established procedures that keep the project going by default.
> - *Salvage costs*: what you could recover by stopping.

> **In class:** The exam-cheating exercise. The instructor asked whether you would cheat in an exam, then whether it would change if your name were not written (only a code ID), and then if you were judged jointly with your batchmates. The slides do not state a lesson. A plain reading (outside the slides) is that the setup changes with anonymity and with joint judgement, which suggests social context shapes behaviour.

## Two puzzles posed in class

**Mandatory CSR.** When a mandatory CSR law was introduced, some firms that were industry leaders in CSR spending reduced their investments. The slides ask only "why?" and give no answer.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** One plausible explanation is that the legal minimum became the new reference point, so voluntary leaders felt they no longer needed to stand out. This is an inference, not the instructor's stated answer.

**Small schools.** A large study of thousands of schools found small schools over-represented by a factor of 4 among the most successful. The Gates Foundation invested over $1.7 billion in creating smaller schools, often by splitting larger ones, followed by other institutions. Then Wainer and Zwerling reported that small schools were over-represented by a factor of 6 among the *least* successful. The slides ask "why?" at the first stage only, and give no answer.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The standard statistical explanation is that small samples vary more, so they produce both the best and the worst results. The lesson is not to infer causation from the top performers alone. The slides do not spell this out.

## The two systems of thinking

**System 1** is automatic judgement. It is always on, almost impossible to turn off, fast and effortless. Its limitation is that there is no thinking behind the act. **System 2** is deliberate reasoning: slow and deliberate, but it prefers a comfortable low-effort mode and often simply endorses System 1's judgement. Its limitation is bounded rationality. So deliberate thinking is not a perfect check on instinct.

The slide adds that the human impulse to do something grinds to a halt when it becomes a hassle, and asks "where does nudge act on?" The slides leave this open.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** A standard answer (outside the slides) is that nudges act on System 1 and on the hassle point, making the desired action easy so that it does not stall.

## Biases and the strategy process

Biases exist, and the implications on the slide are that they affect strategy and decision-making and limit common sense and logical reasoning analogies. (Gloss, not from the slides: we often reason by analogy from past cases, and biases can bend that reasoning.) A title-only slide, "Biases and the Strategy Process", follows with no content.

## Mitigating biases

Three recommendations, abstracted: **awareness** (forewarned is forearmed), **creating processes** (discipline in decision-making; the gloss that this avoids relying on willpower in the moment is mine), and **challenging the biases** (directly confronting or compensating for them).

> **In class:** A process to challenge, from Vermeulen and Sivanathan (2017). Groups argue from an outsider's seat. Group 1: a new CEO and team take over, what would they change? Group 2: a hedge fund has shorted our stock, why did it do so? Group 3: a small group of middle managers has written a memo urging course correction, what would their arguments be? The slides state no lesson. A plausible reading (outside the slides) is that arguing from an outsider's seat lets people voice criticism insiders hold back.

**Amazon's "infamous" meeting rule** is shown as a video with no text on the slides, so its content is not recorded. The next slide, "in a different place...", lists rules: show why he is wrong, ask for clarification, execute the order, and resign if none of these are met. The slide does not say whose rules these are. It asks what other responses are possible in a different context, and leaves the answer open.

## Putting it together

Bounded rationality says we cannot process everything; satisficing is the rational response. Bounded awareness says some available information never reaches us at all. Escalation of commitment shows how many biases combine, with social, project and external factors, to keep a firm in a failing course. System 1 and System 2 describe two modes of thinking (my link: this is why biases are hard to catch), and the three mitigations (awareness, process, challenge) are the toolkit. The outsider exercise is the process version.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Distinguish inattentional blindness, change blindness and focalism with business examples.** Outline: define each; give a short example each; stress that each is a failure to use available information.
2. **Why does escalation of commitment explain the downfall of dominant firms?** Outline: strategic decisions are long-term, cross-functional, path-dependent and irreversible; it is a reinforcement of many biases; add social, project and external factors.
3. **A vignette shows a firm pouring money into a failing project. Identify the factors and advise.** Outline: pick relevant individual, social, project and external factors; recommend awareness, a process, and an outsider challenge.
4. **Compare System 1 and System 2 and say what limits each.** Outline: System 1 is fast, automatic, always on, with no thinking behind the act; System 2 is slow and deliberate, often endorses System 1, limited by bounded rationality. If asked where nudge acts, the slides leave it open; a standard answer (outside the slides) is the System 1 / hassle point.
5. **What is satisficing and why does it follow from bounded rationality?** Outline: constraints on info, time and cognition make the best option unreachable, so people settle for an option that clears the minimum threshold.
