# Sessions 7-9: Organizational Inertia and Responses to Success and Failure

This block asks why organizations keep doing what they have always done, even when the world has moved on, and how they react to high and low performance. The surprising claim is that success can be as dangerous as failure. The deck then gives advice for the top team and consultants, depending on whether the firm is struggling or thriving.

## Action inertia and active inertia

**Action inertia** is the tendency to keep following established patterns of behaviour. **Active inertia**, per the slide, exists when the pull of the past is strong. (Gloss, not from the slides: it means responding to change by doing more of what the firm already does, only harder.) The slides stress that examining success is as necessary as examining failure, because success is what creates the habits that later become a trap.

## Why practices are sticky

The deck gives several reasons.

- **Predictability.** Stable practices are the flip side of predictability. (Gloss, not from the slides: the more reliable a routine is, the harder it is to change.)
- **Path of least resistance and comfort.** Established routines are well known and comfortable.
- **Social complexity, including causal ambiguity.** Often it is unclear why things work, because they arise from interpersonal relations or culture. (Gloss, not from the slides: if you do not know what causes success, you may be afraid to touch anything.)
- **Ingrained culture.** The slides quote Peter Drucker: "Culture eats strategy for breakfast." (My reading: a strategy that conflicts with culture is likely to lose.)
- **Leadership.** As organizations grow, the talents that took them to their current state often become detrimental to future viability. (My gloss: the leaders who built the firm may be the least equipped to change it.)

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** A simple analogy is a restaurant whose famous recipe made it popular. Nobody is sure whether it is the sauce, the chef or the location that works, so no one dares alter the menu, even when tastes change. This analogy is mine, not the instructor's.

## Inertia and evolution

The slides borrow from Darwin, via O'Reilly, Harreld and Tushman: neither strength nor intelligence guarantees survival; only adaptation does. This holds for firms as it does for flora and fauna. So a big, smart, strong company is not safe; what matters is whether it adapts.

## Responses to low performance

When performance is low, two responses appear in the deck.

**Problemistic (problem-solving) search.** Search driven by a problem and aimed at fixing low performance. The slide says it brings innovative behaviour and risk-taking.

**Downward spiral.** A self-perpetuating system with its own momentum. (Gloss, not from the slides: poor performance leads to responses that worsen performance.)

## Responses to high performance

**Complacency.** The slides name attribution bias, overconfidence, and the failure to ask why success happened.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The slides name "attribution bias" without defining it. The usual meaning is giving yourself credit for good outcomes while blaming luck or others for bad ones. Why success breeds comfort is my gloss.

**Slack search.** Search driven by excess resources. The slide says it brings innovative behaviour but limited risk-taking. (Gloss, not from the slides: spare money and people let a firm explore, but comfort gives little reason to bet big.)

The contrast to hold on to: low performance triggers *problemistic search* with real risk-taking, or a *downward spiral*; high performance triggers *slack search* with limited risk-taking, or *complacency*.

> **In class:** The class question. Which of the four responses (problemistic search, downward spiral, complacency, slack search) are influenced by inertia, and which are most common? The slides give no answer. They add only a note on the influence of prospect theory, and the closing line "why do we fall? So we can learn to pick ourselves up."

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Prospect theory says people judge outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, and take more risk to avoid losses than to secure gains. That would explain why the low performer is willing to gamble and the high performer is not. The slides only name the theory here.

## Managerial implications

How can a firm guard against inertia? The slides give four ideas.

1. **A conducive organizational design with institutionalized processes**: introspection (gloss: looking inward on a schedule), leadership turnover (new people bring new views), and experimentation.
2. **Psychological safety**: people must feel safe to speak up and admit mistakes, otherwise problems never surface.
3. **Setting reference points.** Absolute measures are less meaningful. Use historical measures (our own past) and social indicators (peers) to judge performance.
4. **Organizational learning**, with the examples of continuous improvement at Toyota and growth reference points at Microsoft. The slides give no further detail on either.

## Advice for the top team and consultants

Different situations call for different advice.

**Low performers.** The danger is the **panic paradigm**: desperate managers who look for a silver bullet or cut costs erratically. The advice is to stabilize that panic, do rigorous analysis, and redirect them from knee-jerk reactions.

**High performers.** The danger is mistaking current success for future viability. These managers can be complacent and resist intrusions. The advice is to create appropriate **trigger points** (signals that force a rethink) and to highlight blind spots.

> **In class:** The consultant "X-Files". The lesson is that managing the data or the strategy is the easy part; understanding the organizational state is the key. In short: calm the panicked low performer and wake up the comfortable high performer. The slides give no case details beyond this lesson.

**Trouble at Tessei** is introduced at the end as a case, but the deck contains only its title, so there are no details to report. Class activity: find examples of active inertia in an organization that is currently successful.

## Putting it together

Inertia explains why firms stay on the same path: routines are comfortable and culture is hard to change. How that plays out depends on performance. Low performance can trigger a search for solutions or a spiral of panic; high performance can trigger either exploration with slack or complacency. The cure is the same set of habits in both cases: introspection, psychological safety, sensible reference points and learning. The consultant's skill is to read the state of the organization first.
