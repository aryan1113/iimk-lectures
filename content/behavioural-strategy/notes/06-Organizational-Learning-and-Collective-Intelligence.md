# Sessions 14-15: Organizational Learning Context and Wisdom of the Collective

This block asks a double-edged question about groups. In the Everest debrief, teams failed to use what they knew. Here the instructor shows the other side: groups, when designed well, can be smarter than any individual. The sessions cover why context matters for learning, how strategic decisions need diverse knowledge, the wisdom of crowds with its conditions, when crowds fail, and how firms like Google, Threadless and Netflix design around the problems.

## Context matters for organizational learning

The session begins with the **significance of the context**. The deck shows the growth of the **quaternary sector** and asks about the entertainment sector. The quaternary sector is not defined on the slides.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The quaternary sector usually means knowledge-based activity such as information, research, technology and media. The slides give no definition and no answer to the entertainment question, so treat this as background only.

Two slides then list what supports organizational learning ("Supported by:"), each with a chart. The first shows **internet users per 100 inhabitants** from the late 1990s to about 2017, for the developed world, the developing world and globally. The developed world climbs from about 11 to about 81, while the developing world and the global line rise more slowly to roughly 41 and 48. The second shows **global information storage capacity**, charting the shift from analog to digital storage, with 2002 marked as the "beginning of the digital age" and about 94% of storage digital by 2007. The slides then say these lead to **new business models and new ways of organizing**, and show a picture with the question "which popular phrase is represented?" The slides give no answer, and they do not spell out how the charts connect to learning.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** One reading (mine) is that wider internet access and digital storage make information far easier to gather and share, which changes how firms can learn.

## Revisiting Everest: the common information effect

The deck recaps the Everest lesson. Information is either not shared, or shared information is not processed in group decisions. The **common information effect** means information held by more members before the discussion has more influence on team judgement than information held by fewer members. Groups spend too little time on unshared (unique) information, and even when it is shared, they fail to capture it properly in the decision. If you need the full explanation, see the Everest note.

## The other side: the collective intelligence effect

Now the instructor flips the picture. Strategic decisions, such as entering a new country or starting a new business line, cover many perspectives and domains. They need to **integrate divergent knowledge and expertise with a deep understanding of the context**. (Illustration, not from the slides: a decision about a new country might draw on legal, financial, cultural and operational knowledge that no single person holds.) The slide's comment is honest: this is "easy to put in a PowerPoint, hard to put into practice".

### What managers attempt versus what they achieve

A slide contrasts potential with reality in three pairs.

- **Diverse knowledge and expertise brought together** versus the reality of the common information effect interacting with **HIPPO**. The slides use the acronym without expanding it.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** HIPPO is commonly read as "highest paid person's opinion": the senior person's view dominates, so discussion bends toward it.

- **Active discussion and engaged problem-solving** versus **unequal to no participation**, meaning no views are sought from the top and/or no feedback is given from the bottom.
- **Proactive exploration of new ideas and continuous progress** versus **reactive and fragmented efforts**, reacting to competition or the market instead of leading.

The question for the class is: how do you unlock latent cognitive diversity (the different ways people think, which already exist in the firm) and turn it into organizational intelligence?

## Wisdom of the collective

The slogan is "none of us is as smart as all of us". The slides say only "Galton and the fair": the median guess was accurate within 1%.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** In the usual telling (outside the slides), visitors at a fair guessed the weight of an ox, and the median of their guesses was within 1% of the true weight, even though most were not experts.

The deck lists success stories. "Napoleon to Tipu Sultan" and the "BP oil spill" appear as titles only, with no detail, so do not rely on this note for them. Internet-based business models popularized the idea. Examples: **Wikipedia**; **LEGO Ideas**, where the most-voted ideas become products; **Stack Overflow**, **Quora**, **GoFundMe** and **Google reviews**. (Gloss: in each, many people contribute and a system aggregates them.)

## "And yet..." where the collective goes wrong

A slide then lists counter-examples without explaining them. It asks whether Shawshank Redemption is the best movie you have watched (it had an initial box-office failure, and the slide asks which movie held the top spot before), mentions the 2008 rivalry between The Godfather and The Dark Knight, and cites **New Coke (1985)**: a shrinking market gap with Pepsi and 2,00,000 blind taste tests. It also lists community-led village courts (from witch trials to stoning) and betting and financial markets.

The slides do not spell out the outcomes, so the theme is an inference: collective judgement, whether in ratings, taste tests, village courts or markets, can be wrong, biased or even harmful. The slides do not connect this to the conditions below; any link is my reading.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** In the New Coke story, the blind taste tests favoured the new formula, yet the launch is widely remembered as a failure. This hints that what a crowd says in a test can differ from what it wants in real life. The slides do not state the result, so check your class notes.

> **In class:** Class activity. Design a question or challenge, on any topic from business to campus to society, whose solution draws on the collective wisdom of the class, leverages shared knowledge, goes beyond the sum of individual contributions, and gets high participation. The slides give no example answers or stated lesson.

## Conditions for the wisdom of crowds

The slide gives the conditions that make collective wisdom work.

- **Diversity of the crowd.** The slide says: absence of similarities. (Gloss, not from the slides: people with different backgrounds may make different errors that offset each other.)
- **Independence of the individuals.** The slide says: no shared agenda, and not influencing each other. (Gloss: if everyone copies the first speaker, you have one opinion repeated.)
- **Ability to aggregate the information.** There must be some way of combining it, sharing or otherwise.
- **Individual value-claiming should not crowd out group value-creation.** If people use the process to promote themselves or grab credit, the group's output suffers.

A video accompanied the independence point. My link to the Everest note (not stated on the slides): the common information effect and HIPPO can be read as failures of independence and diversity.

## When it does not work

The deck lists: the absence of the three enabling conditions (diversity, independence, aggregation); **lack of expertise** (medicine, law and similar fields where a crowd's guess is no substitute for training); **need for quick decision-making**; **confidentiality or privacy concerns**; and **lack of incentives to participate**.

## Designing around the challenges

Three firms show how to fix specific problems.

- **Google** uses closed betting until a specified time or closing when it asks employees for feedback on new business ideas. The slide's stated purpose is ensuring independence of ideas and avoiding groupthink. (Gloss: bets stay hidden until closing, so people cannot copy each other.)
- **Threadless** filters community-voted designs through internal staff selection. The slide says this gives better integration by reducing noise.
- **Netflix** retro-fits crowd-generated preferences with its internal model, which ensures the **diversity** of the crowd.

The slides label each with a single benefit, so learn the pairing: Google equals independence, Threadless equals less noise, Netflix equals diversity.

## Putting it together

Groups can be smarter or dumber than individuals. The common information effect and HIPPO explain the dumb case; diversity, independence and aggregation explain the smart case. Crowds fail when the conditions are missing, or when expertise, speed, privacy or incentives are the real need. Good design (closed bets, staff filtering, combining crowd data with models) can protect the conditions. The managerial question is how to turn latent cognitive diversity into organizational intelligence.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Why is the collective intelligence effect hard to achieve in organizations?** Outline: strategic decisions need divergent knowledge; reality is the common information effect plus HIPPO, unequal participation, reactive and fragmented efforts.
2. **State the conditions for the wisdom of crowds and give an example of each failing.** Outline: diversity, independence, aggregation, and individual value-claiming not crowding out group value-creation. The slides give no failing examples, so your own examples (e.g. people copying the first speaker, which breaks independence) are outside the slides.
3. **When should you not rely on a crowd?** Outline: missing conditions, lack of expertise (medicine, law), need for speed, confidentiality, lack of incentives.
4. **How do Google, Threadless and Netflix design around crowd challenges?** Outline: Google closed betting for independence; Threadless staff filter to reduce noise; Netflix retro-fits crowd preferences with internal model for diversity.
5. **Compare the Everest finding with the wisdom of the collective.** Outline: both about groups; Everest shows unique information lost; collective wisdom shows aggregated diverse views beat individuals; difference is design (independence, diversity, aggregation).
