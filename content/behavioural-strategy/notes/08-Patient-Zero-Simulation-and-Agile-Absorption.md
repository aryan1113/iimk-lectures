# Sessions 19-20: Patient Zero Simulation, Strategic Decision-Making and Agile Absorption

These sessions debrief the Patient Zero simulation and then use it to ask a hard question: why do results turn out badly even when you make good choices, and what makes some organizations recover? The answer comes through a boxing story (Ali versus Foreman) and the idea of **agile absorption**, a mix of structural and behavioural traits that give a firm resilience under uncertainty.

## The simulation and its debrief

Patient Zero was a decision-making simulation in which teams responded to a series of events. The slides do not describe the setup or give scores ("Results" is a title only), so this note works from the debrief questions.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The slides never say it was an outbreak. That is inferred from the event names ("the infected", "test kits", "vaccine") and from the question "could you save your country?", which suggests each team was running a country. Treat the scenario as inferred; the rest of it is not on the slides.

The debrief questions were: could you save your country, what difficulties did you face in decision-making, what additional information did you wish for, what surprised you about your group's decisions, and how did your team deal with dissenters. The class was also asked which of five events was hardest to respond to, and how it sees its own expertise in making "such decisions". The five events were:

1. A religious pilgrimage.
2. Insight technology test kits.
3. Bilbring and Hite's ineffective vaccine.
4. Zephyr Computing's hidden software.
5. Bombing a cluster of the infected.

> **In class:** The slides do not record the answers, outcomes or the "right" responses. The lessons are in the themes that follow, not in a scoreboard.

## Decision-making in implementation

Why was it so hard? The slide lists: imperfect choices; incomplete information; no previous experience to draw from; pressure from world events or events outside your control; and resolving trade-offs and tensions. The slide gives these examples: **religious freedom versus public health**, **identity challenges** (named without explanation), and the question "is bombing a right way?"

The main message: **implementation requires decisions to be taken despite the absence of certainty over their correctness, while facing very hard challenges, and yet the decisions have significant implications.** In real firms you rarely get to wait for the full picture.

## Should you defer decisions?

The deck asks whether you deferred decisions, and whether you should defer strategic decisions. Its comments are "the world moves on!" and "revisiting a different decision". The slides leave the question open: they do not say you should or should not defer, and they give no explanation beyond those two comments.

## Why are results bad even when choices are good?

This is the central analytic question. The slide breaks it into two parts.

First, **are the choices really good?** Perhaps not. Information may be scarce, contradictory or still developing, and bounded rationality limits how well any group can reason about it (see the Sessions 4-6 note).

Second, **if the choices are good**, results can still disappoint because of:

- **Limited control** over how your solution works when multiple stakeholders are involved in implementation.
- **No control over how the public will respond.**
- **(Almost always) a different context** from the one you planned for.
- **Missing precedent**, meaning no past case to guide you.

So a good decision and a good outcome are different things. The slide titled "the seminal view on strategy implementation" is a diagram, not just a picture. It shows an arrow for **intended strategy** entering from the left and an arrow for **realized strategy** leaving on the right. Between them, a straight line labelled **deliberate strategy** runs from intended to realized. A curved arrow branches downward off the intended strategy toward **unrealized strategy**. A second curved arrow labelled **emergent strategy** joins the line from below and feeds into realized strategy.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The slide shows only the labels and arrows. I recognize it as Mintzberg's strategy diagram, and the plain-English reading is as follows (a gloss, not slide text). *Intended* is what the firm planned. *Deliberate* is the part of the plan that was actually carried out. *Unrealized* is the part of the plan that was dropped or never happened. *Emergent* is what the firm ended up doing without having planned it, as it responded to events. *Realized* is what the firm actually did in the end: deliberate plus emergent. The flow reads: the plan splits into the part executed and the part abandoned, and unplanned actions join the executed part, so the final strategy is not the one that was intended.

The next slide asks: **what organizational attributes help recovery and crafting emergent strategies, so that a firm can rebound from bad decisions?** The slides then move to Ali and Foreman.

## A tale of two heroes: agility versus absorption

Two boxers stand for two ways of coping with a hit.

- **Muhammad Ali**, "The Greatest": "float like a butterfly and sting like a bee". In his prime he epitomized **agility**: avoid damage by moving quickly.
- **George Foreman**, "Big George": "take a licking and keep on ticking". In his prime he epitomized **absorption**: take damage and keep going.

The slide asks: should you be a Muhammad Ali or a George Foreman? > **Extra context (not on the slides):** Translated into a firm (my gloss), the choice is between surviving by being nimble and avoiding shocks, or by being sturdy enough to take them.

> **In class:** The Rumble in the Jungle, 1974. One of the greatest sporting events of the 20th century, with about one billion television viewers and $100m in revenue. The slide shows the fighters' records (40-0 with 37 knockouts and 44-2 with 31 knockouts; which belongs to whom is not stated). The slide asks who won and whether agility wins over absorption, without printing the answer. The next slide implies Ali won, since it describes a KO in the 8th round and says he "maintained trademark agility but also enhanced absorption". On that slide, he was clobbered by sparring partners and used the rope-a-dope: KO in the 8th round. The slide names this **agile absorption**: agility combined with absorption. (That Ali knocked out Foreman is my reading of the slide; the winner is not named on it.)

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The rope-a-dope is the tactic of leaning on the ropes and absorbing blows so the opponent tires. The slides name it without explaining.

## Agile absorption in a firm

The slides ask how a firm achieves agile absorption and list two groups of factors. The one-line reasons attached to the structural factors below are plain-English glosses, not from the slides.

### Structural factors

- **Low fixed costs.** (Gloss: fewer commitments should make it cheaper to survive a revenue fall.)
- **A mix of tangible and intangible resources.** Tangible: raw materials, real estate. Intangible: brand, technology. (Gloss: different resources may hold up differently in different shocks.)
- **Diversified cash flows**, (Gloss: one bad line should not sink the firm.)
- **Customer lock-in or a protected core market.**
- **Slack resources**, meaning maintaining more than the required resources, (Gloss: a buffer to absorb shocks.)

### Behavioural factors

- **Disciplined decision-making**: awareness, processes and constructive challenges (the mitigation toolkit from the bias sessions).
- **Continuous learning and improvement**: being a learning organization.
- **Leadership fluidity**: evolving with the organization (linking to the earlier point that founders' talents can become liabilities).
- **Preventing action inertia**, the downward spiral and complacency (see the inertia note).
- **Rebounding from crisis.**
- **Empowerment and fair process.**

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** A way to remember the two groups (my mapping, not the instructor's): structural factors are what the firm *has* (cash, resources, customers, buffers); behavioural factors are how people in the firm *act* (decide, learn, lead, resist inertia). One could say structure gives capacity to absorb and behaviour gives agility, but the slides do not make that split.

## Key takeaways

- **Embrace reality.** Work around your predicted models and make tough choices.
- **No one-size-fits-all models** in evolving, novel situations with information challenges.
- **Cultivate the ability to respond.** Develop the capacity to become comfortable with the discomfort of tackling "winning and losing", trade-offs and identity challenges.
- **Build resilience** in the face of a catastrophe or an uncertain world through agile absorption, with both structural and behavioural factors.

## Putting it together

The simulation put you in the position of deciding under uncertainty with imperfect information and painful trade-offs. The analysis says bad outcomes are not always bad decisions, because information, control, context and precedent limit what good choices can achieve. Since prediction is unreliable, resilience matters: the Ali story is used to show agility combined with absorption. In firms, that means structural buffers plus behavioural discipline, learning and fluid leadership.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Why can good decisions lead to bad results in strategy implementation?** Outline: information challenges and bounded rationality; limited control with multiple stakeholders; no control of public response; different context; missing precedent; question whether the choices were really good. Optionally use the intended/deliberate/unrealized/emergent/realized diagram.
2. **Explain agile absorption using Ali and Foreman.** Outline: agility versus absorption; Ali kept agility and added absorption (sparring partners, rope-a-dope) and the slides show a KO in round 8; the slides name the combination agile absorption.
3. **List structural and behavioural factors that give a firm agile absorption.** Outline: structural (low fixed costs, tangible/intangible mix, diversified cash flows, lock-in, slack); behavioural (disciplined decisions, learning, leadership fluidity, preventing inertia, empowerment, fair process).
4. **Should strategic decisions be deferred?** Outline: the slides pose this and leave it open ("the world moves on!"). Any verdict is your own argument, outside the slides; give both sides and link to uncertainty.
5. **What did the Patient Zero simulation teach about decisions under uncertainty?** Outline: imperfect information, trade-offs (religious freedom vs public health), identity challenges; decide without certainty; cultivate comfort with discomfort.
