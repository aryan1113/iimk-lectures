## Takeaway 1: nobody is infallible

The first takeaway is that nobody is infallible, whether a captain, a general or a CEO. The slide adds that in such high-pressure moments people tend to fall back on their **tried, tested and perfected routines**. (My reading, not on the slide: the danger is that a routine that has worked before is applied to a different situation, which echoes inertia.)

## Takeaway 2: cognitive humility

The slide names **cognitive humility** and quotes Satya Nadella: **learn-it-all vs know-it-all**, without defining either.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** A plain-English gloss: cognitive humility is awareness that your own knowledge and judgement are limited; a learn-it-all keeps asking and updating, a know-it-all relies on what he already knows. I read it as a personal version of the learning organization and the awareness step in mitigating biases.

## Takeaway 3: emplotment

**Emplotment** is the classification of events and the making of a story out of them. The slide shows how: you can

- arrange events in a certain order;
- decide which events to include and which to leave out;
- stress or emphasize some events;
- provide interpretations selectively;
- ask specific questions.

The slide lists "Mr. KLM", "Captain van Zanten" and "Jaap" in a row, with arrows between them. The slide then asks: **are there really (universal) heroes and villains?** and comments, "often a thin line".

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** My reading of the names (inferred, not stated): the same person may be framed differently depending on the story told, from celebrated figure to the person blamed. The point that a narrative is a construction, where the teller chooses sequence, emphasis and cast, follows from the five emplotment bullets.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** In a business setting, a post-mortem of a failed project can be told as "the manager ignored warnings" or "the team never escalated the risk", using the same facts. Which one is told shapes who gets blamed and what the firm learns. This example is mine.

## Takeaway 4: facts are rarely observed independently

The slide says facts are often not independently observed: the act of observation is already **loaded with a construct of what is being observed**. In other words, **expectations shape your reality**. We see through what we expect to see. The slide names **Joshua Bell** as the example, without telling the story.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Joshua Bell is a famous violinist who once played in a public place as an ordinary busker; most passers-by did not stop. The usual lesson is that people did not expect greatness there, so they did not perceive it. The slides give only the name.

## Takeaway 5: the Rashomon effect

The **Rashomon effect** is contradictory interpretations or descriptions of the same event from different points of view. (Illustration, not from the slides: two witnesses can give conflicting accounts of the same event.)

The slide asks, "implications for business?" and notes it **cuts both ways**. The slides give no further answer, so the following is background only.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** One side: different viewpoints are a problem when departments disagree about what went wrong. The other side: multiple viewpoints are a resource, because together they give a fuller picture, which is the same logic as the diversity of a crowd. The slides only say "cuts both ways".

## Putting it together

The wrap-up brings several course threads together. Leaders are fallible and revert to routines under pressure (bounded rationality, inertia). The remedies are structural and cultural, such as standard communication, crew resource management and less hierarchy (process, psychological safety, constructive challenge), plus a personal attitude of cognitive humility. And because stories and observation are shaped by expectation (emplotment, Rashomon), even our accounts of what happened need to be questioned, which is part of learning from failure.
