# Sessions 4-6: Bounded Rationality, Bounded Awareness and Escalation of Commitment

## Key Concepts

- **Bounded rationality**: decision-making is constrained by information, time and cognitive limits. **Satisficing** follows from it: choose a solution that clears the minimum acceptable threshold ("look for the sharpest needle in the haystack, or one sharp enough to sew?").
- **Bounded awareness**: a specific failure to use information that is available. Types:
  - **Inattentional blindness**: information is available but escapes awareness because attention is consumed by another task (example: a firm racing for market leadership misses the growth in a substitute market).
  - **Change blindness**: failure to notice changes in available information (top management never sees falling employee morale during an acquisition integration).
  - **Focalism**: focusing so much on one event that its importance is overestimated (obsessed with cutting costs, a team misses the aspirational value of owning a car). Kahneman: "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it."
  - People overestimate their awareness and underestimate its bounds (Vandals game exercise).
- **Escalation of commitment**: continuing to commit to a failing course of action. Strategic decisions are long-term, cross-functional, path-dependent and irreversible, so escalation explains most downfalls of dominant firms. It is more than one bias; it is a **reinforcement of many**:
  - *Individual/psychological*: loss aversion, confirmatory bias (outside the slides: seeking confirming evidence), locus of control, gambler's fallacy, personal identification with the project, impression management.
  - *Social*: pluralistic ignorance (outside the slides: everyone privately doubts but assumes others agree), cultural values, group emotions, inter-departmental politics, institutionalized processes.
  - *Project*: size, prior experience, salvage costs.
  - *External*: government policy, stakeholder pressure, competition.
  - Exam-cheating exercise: willingness changes with anonymity and joint judgement (social context shapes behaviour).
- **Puzzles posed:** mandatory CSR law led industry-leading CSR spenders to reduce spending (the slides pose only the question "why?"; the idea that the legal minimum became the reference point is my inference). The **small schools** story: small schools were over-represented 4x among the most successful schools, so Gates and others invested over $1.7bn in smaller schools; Wainer and Zwerling then showed they were over-represented 6x among the least successful. Lesson: small samples produce extremes, so do not infer causation from the top performers alone (explanation is the standard statistics point, not spelled out on slides).
- **Two systems of thinking:** **System 1** is automatic, always on, fast and effortless but involves no deliberate thought. **System 2** is deliberate, slow reasoning; it often just endorses System 1 and is limited by bounded rationality. The impulse to act grinds to a halt when it becomes a hassle; nudges act on this.
- **Biases affect strategy and decision-making** and limit common sense and logical analogies.
- **Mitigating biases** (three abstracted recommendations): **awareness** (forewarned is forearmed), **creating processes** (discipline in decision-making), **challenging the biases** (confront or compensate).
- **Process to challenge (Vermeulen and Sivanathan, 2017):** groups argue as outsiders: a new CEO and team taking over (what would they change?), a hedge fund that shorted our stock (why?), middle managers' memo urging course correction (their arguments?).
