# Introduction to Consumer Behaviour

This opening session sets up the whole course. It explains what consumer behaviour (CB) is, why anyone, as a person and as a manager, should study it, and how business thinking moved from "sell what we make" to "build around what the consumer wants". It also introduces the idea that consumers, not technology, are now the main force changing business. Everything later in the course (perception, personality, emotion, decisions, culture) is a way of understanding the consumer more deeply.

## What consumer behaviour is

The instructor opened with Peter Drucker's line that marketing is the whole business seen from the customer's point of view, and that there is only one valid purpose of a business: to create a customer. The customer determines what the business is. The point is that a firm has no reason to exist without someone willing to buy, so the customer is the starting point, not an afterthought.

The formal definition comes from Solomon (2007). Consumer behaviour is the study of the processes involved when an individual or group **selects, purchases, uses and disposes of** a product, service or experience to satisfy needs. Notice that the definition covers the whole cycle, not just the moment of payment. The slide framed this through six questions about consumption activities: what they buy, why they buy, when they buy, where they buy, how often they buy and how often they use.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Think of a phone. CB is not only "why did she choose this brand", but also how she uses it daily, how often she upgrades, and what she does with the old handset. Each of those is a consumer behaviour a marketer can learn from.

## Why study it

The session gave two reasons.

**At the individual level.** You are a consumer in every role you play, and you spend much of your time shopping, thinking about shopping or talking about it. The goods you buy shape your standard of living. So knowing how you make decisions helps you personally.

**At the managerial level.** The slide asked a question: do marketing managers actually use CB knowledge? It then answered that they definitely should, because successful marketing decisions need extensive information about consumers, and applying CB concepts is critical for success.

The instructor also separated **micro** from **macro** decision making. Micro means understanding consumers so the firm can meet its objectives (the example given was Korean companies and their focus on consumer insights). Macro means that consumers collectively influence society and the economy. One caution from the slides: CB cannot guarantee success, but not applying it leads to disaster. Two quotes reinforced how hard this is. Kroger's Rodney McMullen said "once you see it is too late", and Lever Brothers' CEO Thomas Carroll said that understanding and interpreting the consumer is "easier said than done".

CB exists as its own discipline because marketers realised consumers do not always act as marketing theory predicts. They are unique, so they needed separate study. It is complex and multidimensional, which is why it is both important and difficult. The slides add that CB matters for all types of organisation, that firms must understand it to stay in time and in trend, and that this is more so in India.

## How business orientation evolved

The slides show a three-step evolution, each defined by where profit is expected to come from:

- **Sales concept:** profit through sales volume.
- **Product concept:** profit through quality.
- **Modern marketing (consumer-oriented) concept:** profit through customer satisfaction.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Presumably volume and quality are firm-side ideas while satisfaction is judged by the customer.

The slide summarises the consumer-oriented idea as giving the **right products to consumers**, not finding the right consumers for the company's product.

This focus has spread beyond products to services: hospitals became patient-centric, schools and universities student-centric, and banks focused on account holders.

## What is changing in India

The instructor listed the forces reshaping the Indian consumer. Drivers of change: a booming economy (racing towards a 5 trillion economy), a young market (about half the population below 25), smaller and nuclear households, DINKs (double income, no kids), demand for small SKUs (small pack sizes), rising awareness and westernisation. Consumer trends that follow: **affordable indulgence** (people judge by the monthly EMI, not the absolute price), time pressure, health consciousness, upgrading rather than lifelong possession, and convenience as a new part of consumer psychology.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The EMI point means a Rs 60,000 phone feels like "Rs 3,000 a month", so the barrier to buying drops even though total cost does not.

## Consumer orientation and customer centricity

Two ideas sit close together and are easy to confuse.

**Consumer (customer) orientation** is creating value for customers in a way competitors cannot. In an organisation it appears as a culture made of three parts: consumer orientation, competitor orientation and inter-functional coordination (departments working together rather than in silos).

**Customer centricity** is a core value of the organisation, an ideology and not an isolated event, where every step starts and ends with customer satisfaction. The slides list how to practise it:

- use customer data to understand customers better;
- commit to customer success;
- engage with customers from the beginning;
- show commitment from the top down;
- design processes and policies from the customer's point of view;
- measure what matters to customers;
- encourage customer innovation and co-creation.

The slides also describe market sensing as needing a mindset that is mindful, meaning being present to sense what will unfold in future.

**The 1990s shift.** One of the great transformations was from a company-driven economy to a consumer-driven one. The slide contrasts push versus pull and excess demand with little supply versus less demand with more supply. When supply exceeds demand, customers can choose, so firms must win them. The result was that institutions became **market-driven rather than marketing-driven**: they integrate customers into product development, give them exactly what they need, offer excellent service throughout the relationship and let the customer guide decisions across the institution.

## Curiosity as a marketer's virtue

The instructor treated curiosity as a trait every marketer needs. It is the gap between what we know and what we want to know, or between knowledge and understanding. It may not pay off immediately, but it yields deep insight when needed, broadens and sharpens the intellect and drives continuous improvement. The slides call it a great virtue alongside empathy and business acumen.

The tools for building it are practical habits: keep updating your knowledge; listen extensively without judgement (the Mark Twain line about having two ears and one tongue); always ask questions; observe consumers with fresh eyes; seek new experiences in new places; and embrace the unexpected.

## Who is the disruptor?

The slides listed Borders (2011), Nokia (2013) and J.Crew (2017) and asked: who is disrupting, technology or the consumer? The answer on the slide is the **consumer**. The slide then says companies must move from being firm-centric or competitor-centric to consumer-centric, because consumers are changing the business landscape.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Borders, Nokia and J.Crew are used on the slide only as names and dates. The reading that each failed to keep up with changing consumer behaviour is the slide's overall argument, not a case analysis given in the deck.

## The consumer value chain and showrooming

The **consumer value chain** is the series of activities a consumer performs to satisfy a need: need arousal, information search, evaluation of alternatives, choice and purchase. For ages, the whole chain happened inside one store.

What is new is **decoupling**: the steps are now split across places and sellers. In **showrooming**, a shopper visits a physical store, scans barcodes on a smartphone, compares prices, buys from the e-commerce site with the lowest price and gets it delivered. The store only serves one part of the chain, looking at the product. The slide's example was the **Best Buy case**. 

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Our reading: a store that only serves as a showroom bears the cost while another seller makes the sale. The slide gives only "Eg: Best Buy case".

## Putting it together

A business exists to create customers (Drucker); CB studies how they select, buy, use and dispose. Firms moved from sales to product to consumer orientation, helped by customer centricity and curiosity, while the disruption examples and showrooming show consumers now drive change.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Define consumer behaviour and explain why a manager should study it.** Give Solomon's definition (select, purchase, use, dispose to satisfy needs). Give individual reasons and managerial reasons, and mention micro versus macro decisions. Add that CB cannot guarantee success but ignoring it leads to disaster.
2. **Trace the evolution of marketing orientation.** Sales concept (profit via volume), product concept (via quality), modern marketing concept (via satisfaction). Explain the 1990s move from push and excess demand to pull and excess supply, and market-driven versus marketing-driven.
3. **What is customer centricity and how would a firm practise it?** Define it as a core value, not an event. Use the seven practices (data, success, early engagement, top-down commitment, customer-view processes, measuring what matters, co-creation).
4. **Explain showrooming and the decoupling of the consumer value chain, using Best Buy.** Describe the five-step chain, how steps now separate, and what the store loses. Best Buy is named without detail on the slide.
5. **Who is the disruptor, technology or the consumer? Argue with examples.** Answer is the consumer (Borders, Nokia, J.Crew). The slide says firms must shift from firm- or competitor-centric to consumer-centric.
