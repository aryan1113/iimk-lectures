# Personality

Why does one person queue for the newest gadget while another keeps the same phone for years? Personality is the lecture's answer. This session defines personality, walks through the main theories, then catalogues the specific consumer traits that marketers use to predict what people buy and which ad appeal works on them. It finishes with materialism and compulsion, ethnocentrism and brand personality.

## What personality is

**Personality** is the inner psychological characteristics that both determine and reflect how a person responds to his or her environment, in other words how they think and act. The slide gives three features of its nature:

- It reflects **individual differences**: no two people are identical.
- It is **consistent and enduring**.
- It **can change**.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Consistency is what makes personality useful for prediction, and "can change" presumably reflects life events and maturing; the slide gives only the three statements.

## Three theories of personality

**Freudian theory.** Unconscious needs or drives are at the heart of human motivation. *(Plain-English gloss, not from the slides: people may not know why they really buy something.)*

**Neo-Freudian theory.** Social relationships are fundamental to the formation and development of personality. It shifts the focus from inner instincts to how we relate to other people.

**Trait theory.** A trait is any distinguishing, relatively enduring way in which one individual differs from another. This is the most practical for marketers because it measures specific characteristics. The slide notes that personality is linked to how consumers make choices, to consumption of a broad product category, and to a specific brand.

## Horney's CAD theory

Horney, using the context of child-parent relationships, classified people into three types:

- **Compliant:** wants to be loved, wanted and appreciated by others ("moves toward others" is Horney's usual wording, not on the slide).
- **Aggressive:** moves against others. Competes, wants to excel and win admiration.
- **Detached:** moves away from others. Wants independence, self-sufficiency and freedom from obligations.

The slides give the three definitions but no brand examples.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** One way to remember it: if a group invites you out, the compliant person goes because they want to be liked, the aggressive person goes to be the centre of attention, and the detached person stays home. How each type might respond to an ad is the reader's inference, not slide content.

## Consumer traits

The deck lists six traits: innovativeness, dogmatism, social character, need for uniqueness, optimum stimulation level and variety-novelty seeking. Several are linked to each other.

**Consumer innovativeness** is the degree to which consumers are receptive to new products, services or practices. This is the base idea that most of the others explain.

**Dogmatism** is a trait reflecting the degree of rigidity a person shows toward the unfamiliar and toward information contrary to their own established beliefs. The slide draws two consequences:

- Consumers **low in dogmatism** (open-minded) are more likely to prefer innovative products to established or traditional alternatives.
- **Highly dogmatic** consumers tend to be more receptive to ads for new products or services that carry an appeal from an authoritative figure. *(Plain-English gloss, not from the slides: authority may reassure those who distrust the unfamiliar.)*

**Social character** describes where people look for direction.

| | Inner-directed | Other-directed |
|---|---|---|
| Where direction comes from | Own inner values | Other people |
| Innovators? | More likely | Less likely |
| Preferred ads | Product features and benefits | Social acceptance |

So to sell to an inner-directed consumer you argue the merits of the product; to an other-directed consumer you show that people will approve.

**Need for uniqueness** is the desire to avoid appearing to conform to the expectations or standards of others. *(Plain-English gloss: they avoid what everyone else buys.)*

**Optimum stimulation level (OSL)** measures the amount of novelty or complexity individuals seek in their experiences. High-OSL consumers accept risky and novel products more readily than low-OSL consumers. **Variety-novelty seeking** is a similar trait that measures a consumer's degree of variety seeking; the slide lists **sensation seeking (SS)** as an example under it: a need for varied, novel and complex sensations and experiences, with willingness to take **physical and social risks** for them.

## Cognitive personality factors

These are about how people process information.

**Need for cognition (NC)** is a person's craving for, and enjoyment of, thinking. The consequence for advertising: consumers **high in NC** respond to ads rich in product-related information, while those **low in NC** are more attracted to background or peripheral aspects of an ad (the music, the models, the setting).

**Visualisers versus verbalisers** captures preferred format: visualisers prefer information presented visually, verbalisers prefer it in words. *(Plain-English gloss: match format to audience.)*

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** A chart-heavy infographic suits a visualiser, a detailed written explainer suits a verbaliser. The slides only define the two types.

## From materialism to compulsion

The slides present a spectrum of how strongly people are attached to consumption.

**Consumer materialism** is the extent to which a person is "materialistic". The slide's description: materialistic people value acquiring and showing off possessions, are particularly self-centred and selfish, seek lifestyles full of possessions, and have many possessions that do not lead to greater happiness. (The slide's last point: many possessions do not lead to greater happiness.)

**Fixated consumption** is a deep interest in a particular object or product category. Fixated consumers are willing to go to considerable lengths to get items in that category and dedicate a considerable amount of discretionary time and money to searching for them. The examples are **collectors and hobbyists**. *(Gloss, not from the slides: intense but not harmful.)*

**Compulsive consumption** is when consumers are "addicted" or "out of control", and their actions may have damaging consequences for themselves and for people around them.

*(Gloss, not from the slides: the difference is control and damage.)*

## Consumer ethnocentrism

**Ethnocentric consumers feel it is wrong to purchase foreign-made products.** The marketing implication on the slide: they can be targeted by stressing **nationalistic themes**. For a domestic brand, an appeal to buying from your own country speaks directly to their belief.

## Brand personality

Just as people have personalities, brands are seen as having them. The deck lists five dimensions:

- **Sincerity:** down to earth, honest, wholesome, cheerful.
- **Excitement:** daring, spirited, imaginative, up to date.
- **Competence:** reliable, intelligent, successful.
- **Sophistication:** upper-class and charming.
- **Ruggedness:** outdoorsy and tough.

The slides list the dimensions without naming brands.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Presumably the idea is that consumers choose brands whose personality fits their own.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Reading a brand's ads and tone and asking "which of the five adjectives would a person use for it?" is a practical way to classify it in an exam answer. Brand examples for each dimension are for you to supply.

## Putting it together

Theories describe personality; the traits predict which products people accept and which appeals persuade them; materialism, fixation and compulsion describe how far consumption takes over; ethnocentrism and brand personality extend the idea to origin and brands.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Define personality and describe its nature.** Inner psychological characteristics that determine and reflect responses to the environment; individual differences, consistent and enduring, but can change. Name the three theories.
2. **Explain Horney's CAD theory and what each type wants.** Compliant wants to be loved and appreciated; aggressive moves against (competes, admiration); detached moves away (independence, freedom from obligation).
3. **Match an advertising appeal to a consumer type.** Inner-directed: features and benefits; other-directed: social acceptance; high NC: information-rich; low NC: peripheral cues; low dogmatism: innovative products; high dogmatism: authority appeals.
4. **Distinguish materialism, fixated consumption and compulsive consumption.** Materialism is valuing and showing off possessions; fixated is deep interest in a category (collectors, hobbyists); compulsive is addiction with damaging consequences.
5. **Classify a brand using the five brand-personality dimensions and explain how ethnocentrism could be used in its marketing.** Name the dimension with its descriptors; state that ethnocentric consumers think foreign goods are wrong to buy, so use nationalistic themes.
