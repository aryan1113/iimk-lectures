# Emotion in Consumer Behaviour

Much of what people buy is decided by how they feel, not by calculation. This session defines emotion, lists the basic emotions and how they arise, then looks at how marketers deliver and use emotion, including the uncomfortable cases of fear, shame and guilt. It closes with evidence from a brain-imaging study that the emotions around a brand change how we respond to it.

## What emotion is

**Emotion** is a subjective feeling state caused by a person consciously or subconsciously evaluating an event that is relevant to a goal (a concern). The key is *goal relevance*. When the event **advances** the goal, a positive, pleasant emotion appears; when the goal is **impeded**, a negative, unpleasant one.

## The eight primary emotions

The deck lists eight primary emotions, each a family of related feelings:

- **Anger:** fury, outrage, irritability, hostility, resentment.
- **Sadness:** grief, sorrow, gloom, despair, loneliness.
- **Fear:** anxiety, apprehension, nervousness, dread, panic.
- **Joy:** enjoyment, happiness, relief, delight, pride, thrill.
- **Interest:** acceptance, friendliness, trust, kindness, affection, love.
- **Surprise:** shock, astonishment, amazement, wonder.
- **Disgust:** contempt, disdain, scorn, aversion, revulsion.
- **Shame:** guilt, embarrassment, remorse, regret.

**Secondary emotions** are an emotional reaction *to* an emotion: feeling shame when you get angry, feeling angry when you feel shame (hurt feelings), or feeling fear when you get angry (perhaps because you were punished for anger earlier). Emotions can also be grouped as active or passive positive, and active or passive negative.

## How emotions occur: the multicomponent model

An emotion is described as an episode of **coordinated changes in several components** in response to a significant internal or external event:

- **Cognitive appraisal:** evaluating the event or object (seeing a snake).
- **Bodily symptoms:** a rapid heartbeat.
- **Action tendencies:** the urge to run away.
- **Expression:** facial or vocal.
- **Feelings:** the subjective experience.

The slide's snake example illustrates cognitive appraisal only. Emotions also have **intensity** (strength), **direction** and **quality** (content). They are part of everyday life, many and varied, and some are culture-specific.

Marketers stay curious about emotion because identifying it is hard, articulating it is harder, and it affects consumers' and prospects' lives.

## Delivering emotion in the marketplace

There are two ways:

- **Product emotions:** delivered by the product's own attributes.
- **Associated emotions:** generated independently of the product (through words, images and contexts) and linked to the product **through repetition**.

> **In class:** A **Surf Excel ad** was shown (the slide notes "we pay attention to relationship"), and the slides say ad-delivered emotion is associated emotion linked to the product by repetition. Two further ads followed with the question "what emotions are elicited in you?"; no answer is given.

The slide lists ten features of emotional marketing: storytelling, visuals and imagery, music and sound, personalisation, humour, social proof, cause marketing, nostalgia, fear and urgency, and empathy. These are the levers firms pull to create associated emotions.

## Fear appeals and Protection Motivation Theory

The slide shows a mammogram fragment: those given one message ("if a cancer has spread, it is more likely to be fatal") were more likely to get a mammogram within 12 months than those given another ("if a cancer has not spread, it is less likely to be fatal"). The start of the sentence is cut off on the slide. Reading these as loss-framed versus gain-framed, with loss-framed doing better, is our inference from the fragment. A sunscreen slide gives the same contrast in wording (gain: sunscreen keeps skin healthy and young-looking; loss: not using it raises skin cancer risk), but the slide states **no result** for it.

The explanation is Rogers' **Protection Motivation Theory** (1975). People are motivated to protect themselves from physical, psychological and social threats. A fear appeal starts a **coping appraisal** built on four variables:

1. **Severity** of the threat.
2. **Probability** of it happening if no adaptive behaviour is performed.
3. **Availability of a coping response** (a solution).
4. The person's **ability to carry out** that coping behaviour.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Plain-English reading: fear alone is not enough; the person must also feel there is something they can do. Without a workable solution, fear can cause avoidance.

## Shame and guilt in marketing

The slides ask for further examples where shame, embarrassment, guilt and fear are used, then give cases.

**Shame and embarrassment: condoms and sexual wellness.** Products people may feel awkward buying. The examples shown are discreet delivery and D2C brands (My Muse, Boldcare, Fix My Curls), linked to the growth of niche D2C brands in smaller cities via quick commerce. *(Gloss, not from the slides: discreet delivery presumably removes the awkwardness of buying.)*

**Baby products (diapers, infant formula).** The slide calls some claims **vaguely deceptive** ("our brand is the best") and others **outright deceptive** (infant formula is better than breast milk). It poses the ethical question of using parental anxiety, without further discussion.

**Fairness creams (Fair and Lovely)** are given as an example of guilt and fear.

> **In class (case): the funeral industry and the Beyond campaign.** Students were asked for their personal reaction. The slide explains why this market is vulnerable: social norms make it hard to price shop, bargain or buy the cheapest product; the buyer is purchasing at a vulnerable time and wants to get it over with quickly; buyers have incomplete knowledge, made worse by a lack of transparency about products and prices; and sellers can **guilt** the buyer into a more expensive version. **Beyond** aimed to break the social norms that discourage price comparison. *(Gloss, not from the slides: the campaign presumably gives buyers back the power to compare.)*

## Implications for consumer behaviour

Emotion builds **desire and urge**, drives **hedonic** purchase behaviour (buying for pleasure), **impulse buying** and **variety seeking**, and builds **loyalty**. The marketer's role is to use emotions to influence decisions in three ways, which the slide frames as three "factors":

- Reduce the **thinking factor**: make the process easy, give an optimal choice and cut unnecessary information.
- Reduce the **risk factor**: make the consumer see the product or store as safe.
- Raise the **impression factor**: make the encounter with the product, brand or store memorable.

## Why emotion matters: the Coke versus Pepsi scan

> **In class (experiment):** Researchers at Emory University served people Coke and Pepsi while in an fMRI scanner. When the brands were **not identified**, they saw a consistent response. When the brands **were identified**, the **limbic system** (brain structures linked with emotion, memory and unconscious processing) showed greater activity.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The slide's heading is that this is why emotions matter to marketers; our reading is that brand identity adds emotional and memory response beyond the drink itself.

## Putting it together

Emotion is a goal-linked feeling with several components. Marketers use product or associated emotions, fear appeals (Protection Motivation Theory) and, carefully, shame and guilt, aiming to reduce thinking and risk and raise impression. The Emory scan shows brand identity changes the limbic response.
