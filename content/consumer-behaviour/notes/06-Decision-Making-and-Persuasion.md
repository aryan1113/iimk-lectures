# Decision Making, Nudging & Persuasion

Consumers like to think they choose rationally. This session argues they do not, and that the departures can be anticipated. It runs from a bargain-hunting exercise that introduces two kinds of utility, through heuristics and information overload, to three tools for marketers (choice architecture, nudging, persuasion), with seven worked persuasion examples.

## Two kinds of utility

> **In class (exercise):** Adapted from Richard Thaler. Maya wants a quilt for her double bed. Regular prices are $300 (king), $250 (queen) and $200 (double). At the store all three are $150. Which size does she buy? The slide's answer: she buys the extra-large because the transaction utility was very high, and in doing so she "misbehaved" by ignoring acquisition utility.

The slides define the two utilities:

- **Acquisition utility** is the rational value of the purchase, its true value through use of the product.
- **Transaction utility** is the perceived quality of the deal at the point of purchase: the difference between the price on offer and the price you would normally expect to pay.

Shoppers get hooked on transaction utility because a great bargain makes you feel instantly victorious and happy. Great deals tempt us into purchases of little use, which end up in cupboards or lofts. The slide quotes Thaler: irrationality, biases and idiosyncrasies are at the heart of decision making. The implication given: marketers can use the dual utility to design promotional sales and ask "what will dial up transaction utility?"

> **In class:** A rating questionnaire asked how much you agree with statements about enjoying promotional deals (for example, "promotional deals have made me buy products I normally would not").

## How people really decide

Decision makers generally deviate from rational decision making and want simplifying strategies, called **heuristics**. The reason given: of the huge amount of information striking us each second, about **50 pieces are captured and 7 processed**, so the brain uses shortcuts. The marketer's role, per the slide, is to refrain from normative claims and prescriptive suggestions to customers and to **influence**, meaning guide attitude positively toward a target.

**Information overload.** Information is dense, too much, ambiguous, contradictory and uncertain, leaving consumers unsure what to believe, with risk in believing or not believing. **Too many options.** The slide contrasts a few Indian car models (Ambassador, Premier Padmini, Maruti) with thousands now: options are good, but we get **paralysed by them and choose suboptimally**. The solution: help consumers make sense of the information; presenting crucial data in an accessible style makes processing easier.

> **In class:** Two advertisements were compared, one with information given randomly and one with only the key information given or highlighted.

## Choice architecture

Marketers anticipate behaviour and overcome cognitive barriers through **choice architecture**, the design of the environment: how to present choices and the effects of presentation on decisions. The slide's illustration: "Can I smoke while I pray?" versus "Can I pray while I smoke?", with the takeaway that what you say is not the whole story, how you say it matters.

## Nudging

Humans make predictable errors, so marketers can anticipate them and devise strategies to reduce the error rate. **Nudging** is influencing choices in a way that makes choosers **better off, as judged by themselves**.

> **In class (example):** Amsterdam airport (Thaler). Spillage around urinals is an age-old problem. Etching the image of a fly in the urinal cut spillage by 80%. The slide's explanation: give men a target and they cannot help but aim at it; it also saves clean-up costs.

## Persuasion

**Persuasion** focuses on *how to say it*, a psychological influence. The aim is a higher probability of a "yes". The information or offer does not change; only the way it is presented does, using research on how people process information, judge and evaluate outcomes. The slides call it a tool and a frame.

### 1. Loss framing

> **In class:** A power company rep gave free home energy audits door to door, then pitched insulation. Half the homeowners heard "If you insulate your home, you will save Rs X per day." The other half heard "If you fail to insulate your home, you will lose Rs X per day." A much higher percentage said yes to the second.

The principle is **loss framing**. The slide adds an HIV screening example showing 63% versus 23% (it does not label which wording is which; reading it as loss versus gain framing, loss being higher, is our inference) and repeats the mammogram and sunscreen wording from the emotion lecture. The explanation is Rogers' **Protection Motivation Theory** (see Emotion). **Caution:** it works well for one-time purchases but may backfire if the purchase is regular.

### 2. Door-in-the-face

> **In class:** Students posing as juvenile detention staff asked passers-by to escort young offenders on a Saturday zoo trip: 17% agreed. In another version they first asked for an extreme favour (two hours a week for three years as a counsellor), which everyone refused, then asked for the zoo escort, and 50% agreed.

The slide names this the **door-in-the-face (DITF)** strategy: a big request followed by a smaller one.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The plain-English reason is that after refusing a big ask, people feel the asker has compromised, so saying no again feels awkward.

### 3. Status quo bias and defaults

In Germany nobody is a donor by default and people must opt in: about 12% have joined. In Austria all are in the pool by default and can opt out: 99.98% stay. Attitudes are apparently less powerful than the **default policy**: the power of inertia, **status quo bias**. A slide on a chocolate launched in 1980 versus 2018 is labelled "longevity bias" with no explanation, so its meaning is left open.

### 4. Reciprocity

A questionnaire with no incentive got the lowest response; promising Rs 500 raised it modestly; Rs 50 attached upfront got by far the most. The slide names this the **power of reciprocity**.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The idea is that a small gift received first creates a felt obligation to respond, which can beat a larger promised reward.

### 5. Aggregate losses, disaggregate gains

Which makes you happier: finding one Rs 200 note, or Rs 100 on one day and another Rs 100 the next? Which makes you unhappier: losing one Rs 200 note, or Rs 100 on two days? The slide names the principle only: aggregate losses, disaggregate gains.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The usual reading is that two separate gains feel better than one combined gain, and one combined loss hurts less than two separate ones, so give good news in pieces and bad news in one go.

### 6. Social proof

> **In class:** Infomercials traditionally said "Operators are waiting, please call now." A company changed three words to "If operators are busy, please call again." The slide asks for the result without stating it, then names the **power of social proof**.

Social proof has the greatest effect when the prospect is **uncertain** and when they see **similar others** using the product ("those people are like me, so I'm the type who would use it"). Hotel towel signs saying "most people reuse their towel at least once during their stay" raised reuse by over 26%.

> **In class (warning):** An Arizona national forest sign said theft of petrified wood was destroying the heritage at 14 tons a year. Marked pieces were stolen more with the sign than without (7.92% versus 2.92%). The slide's lesson: telling people what most others do influences them to do the same, whether positive or negative.

### 7. Effort illusion

Unpleasant tasks seem more effortful, not because of the task but the unpleasantness a person associates with it (the slide's illustration: a teenager talking to a mother versus a boyfriend or girlfriend). Marketing application: shopping is a chore for some and enjoyable for others, so make the store useful for both, with **quick checkout** for the chore shopper and a **leisure experience** for the enjoyable shopper.

**Benefit:** effective framing reduces barriers and creates a feel-good subjective experience compared with the objective outcome.

## Putting it together

People chase deals, rely on shortcuts and freeze under overload. Marketers respond by designing presentation (choice architecture), nudging, and persuading by changing only how things are said: framing, requests, defaults, gifts, grouping, social cues and store design.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Distinguish acquisition from transaction utility with the quilt example.** Acquisition is true use value; transaction is perceived deal quality versus expected price. Maya buys the largest size for the deal.
2. **Why heuristics, and what does overload do?** About 50 captured and 7 processed per second, so shortcuts; too much information and choice paralyses and gives suboptimal choices; highlight the key data.
3. **Define nudging, choice architecture and persuasion.** Nudge: steer to choices better by the chooser's own judgement (fly in the urinal). Choice architecture: how choices are presented. Persuasion: change presentation, not the offer.
4. **Name the principle in a vignette.** Loss framing (insulation), door-in-the-face (zoo), status quo (organ donation), reciprocity, aggregate losses and disaggregate gains, social proof (towels, petrified wood), effort illusion.
5. **Design a persuasive appeal and note its limits.** Pick a principle and framing. Limits from the slides: loss framing may backfire on regular purchases; social proof backfires if it advertises a bad norm.
