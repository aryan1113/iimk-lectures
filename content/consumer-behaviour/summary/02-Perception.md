# Perception & Sensory Marketing

## Key Concepts

- **Perception** is the process by which an individual selects, organises and interprets stimuli into a meaningful picture of the world: selection → organisation → interpretation.
- **Selective attention**: consumers perceive only a fraction of stimuli. Attention is likelier when the stimulus relates to a current need, is anticipated, or deviates strongly. Selection depends on prior experience (expectations) and motives. **Perceptual defence** screens out threatening stimuli (words vs images on smoking warnings); **blocking** is avoiding ads (recording programmes).
- **Perceptual organisation (Gestalt)**: figure and ground, grouping, closure (the mind completes incomplete figures).
- **Stereotyping**: biased pictures people carry about stimuli. **Descriptive terms** shape perception ("succulent Italian seafood fillet" vs "seafood fillet"). **Halo effect**: overall evaluation from one or few dimensions; a prestigious product image rubs off on other products under the same name.
- **Elements of perception**:
  - *Absolute threshold*: minimum stimulus detectable; *sensory adaptation* makes people stop noticing; fix by raising or lowering sensory input.
  - *Differential threshold / j.n.d.*: the minimal detectable difference. **Weber's law**: the needed change is relative to the intensity of the initial stimulus. Marketers keep negative changes below the j.n.d. (price rise, smaller sachets, coffee-chicory mix) and make improvements clearly above it (bigger size, lower price, new pack).
  - *Subliminal perception*: below consciousness. Vicary's 1957 popcorn/Coke claim has not been backed by research; evidence of behaviour change is absent, though subliminal cues may influence affect.
- **Positioning**: against competition, on a specific benefit, repositioning.
- **Colour associations**: red (energy, excitement, hunger), green (nature, freshness), orange (youth, call to action), yellow (happiness, optimism, attention; stronger with red), pink (romance, feminine), black (luxury, authority).
- **Sensory marketing**: engaging the senses to influence perception, judgement and decision; products "speak" through design (Kinder Joy) rather than firms talking. Relies on **embodied cognition** (bodily sensations drive decisions unconsciously). Why it works: breaks ad clutter via the product itself, is not seen as ordinary marketing so resists less, processing is visceral. Benefits: adds value, shapes the holistic experience, builds loyalty (lasting memory effect), reduces price competition. Sensation is the biochemical response of receptors; perception is awareness of it.

## Why This Matters

Explains why identical products are judged differently and gives marketers levers (thresholds, design, colour, senses) that work below conscious deliberation.

## Relevance for Exams / Cases

Apply Weber's law to a price or pack change, name the Gestalt principle in an ad, and argue the case for sensory marketing.
