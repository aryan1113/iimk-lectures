# Session 1: What Exactly Counts as E-Commerce?

This opening session sets the lens for the whole course. It asks a deceptively simple question, what counts as e-commerce, and then pushes you to look past the shop window to the machinery behind it. If you remember one thing, remember that a customer sees a simple "Buy now" button while the firm has to make a dozen hard things true behind it.

## A definition built on a boundary

The session defines e-commerce as a sale or purchase in which the **order is placed through a computer network, using a method designed for ordering**. Notice what the definition leaves open. The scope is broad: goods and services, and the parties can be businesses, consumers, governments or other organisations. Payment and delivery can happen online or offline. So the only thing that decides whether something is e-commerce is the **boundary test**: how was the order placed?

The reasoning is that the definition has to be sharp enough to classify messy real cases. If we counted every digital touch as e-commerce, almost everything would qualify. By fixing on the order, we get a test we can apply the same way every time.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Think of a restaurant. A phone call to book a table and a booking through an app both end with you eating in the same room. Only the second is an order placed through a network by a method designed for it. The meal is identical; the boundary is the ordering channel.

## E-commerce versus e-business

These two words are easy to blur. **E-commerce is a transaction**: a digitally ordered exchange of goods or services across organisational or market boundaries. **E-business is the wider organisational system**: digital technologies used to run and coordinate processes whether or not a sale takes place. The slides' contrast is clear. A grocery order placed in an app is e-commerce. Using software to forecast inventory is e-business, because no customer order is involved, yet it still matters to the firm.

Why does the distinction matter? The customer only ever sees the transaction. Managers, however, have to design the larger system that makes the transaction possible. Keep that split in mind: customer sees e-commerce, manager owns e-business.

## Start with your last online purchase

> **In class:** The session opened with a warm-up. Recall one memorable online purchase (for delight, frustration, surprise or regret) and write three things: what you saw or did, what you expected to happen next, and **what the organisation must have done behind the screen**. Share with the person beside you. It is a "fresh start" prompt: the point of the whole session is that last question.

## The order defines it, but the journey is bigger

Although the order is the test, the session insists managers understand the whole journey around it. The order itself (via a website, app, EDI, a message or an agent) sits at the boundary. Around it are:

- **Discovery**: search, social media, creators, even a store visit.
- **Payment**: UPI, card, wallet, cash on delivery.
- **Fulfilment**: a download, pickup, a courier.
- **Service and repeat purchase**: support, returns, coming back.

The slide labels the stages: order (website, app, EDI, message, agent), fulfilment (download, pickup, courier, local delivery), repeat (subscription, reorder, reminder, agent), discovery (search, social feed, creator, store visit), payment (UPI, card, wallet, bank, cash on delivery) and service (chat, call centre, store, self-service). The slide asks which stages matter managerially, and leaves that open. Each of these can be online or offline. That is exactly why a boundary test is needed: the journey crosses worlds, but the classification looks at one moment only.

## Boundary cases: apply one test

The class sorted ten boundary cases into **EC** (e-commerce), **Not EC** or **Depends**. The single test on the slide: **was an order for a good or service placed through a digital network?** The slides repeat two warnings: do not confuse digital *discovery* or digital *payment* with digital *ordering*, and do not confuse "digital" with "commercial".

**"Depends"** is not a vague middle answer. It means the facts given are not enough to apply the test, because one missing fact (for example, how exactly the order was placed or authorised) would tip the case either way. That is why every case also needs "what fact would change your answer?".

> **In class:** Groups had 8 minutes. For each case, write three lines: your judgement, the boundary test you applied, and one missing fact that could change your answer ("Case # is ___ because ___"). The ten cases:
> 1. **Instagram to phone call**: sees a product on Instagram, calls the shop, pays by UPI, uses a local courier.
> 2. **WhatsApp plus COD**: messages a grocery list to a store and pays cash on arrival.
> 3. **Netflix renewal**: a subscription renews automatically without a fresh customer action.
> 4. **Web research to store**: compares laptops online but places the final order at a physical shop.
> 5. **EDI replenishment**: a retailer's system automatically sends a replenishment order to a supplier.
> 6. **Ride booked in an app**: requests and pays digitally; the transport is delivered offline.
> 7. **UPI transfer**: one friend sends money to another; no good or service is exchanged.
> 8. **AI suggests, then store**: an AI assistant recommends a product; the customer later buys it in a shop.
> 9. **Smart fridge reorders**: a connected appliance places an authorised grocery order automatically.
> 10. **Creator to affiliate order**: a viewer follows a creator's link and completes an online purchase.
>
> The slides give no answer key, so none is stated here. The lesson is to apply the one test, separate discovery and payment from ordering, and name the deciding fact.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Applying the test myself, as an illustration only: cases 8 and 4 look like not-EC (the order happened in a shop), 6 and 10 look like EC (order placed digitally), and 7 is not commerce at all. Cases 2, 3, 5 and 9 are the ones that turn on a missing fact, such as whether the message or the standing authorisation counts as an order placed by a method designed for ordering. Treat these as my reading, not the instructor's.

## Eight distinctive characteristics

The session lists eight features that make e-commerce different from traditional commerce. Learn each with its one-line meaning:

1. **Ubiquity**: available anywhere, anytime. The market becomes a "marketspace", lowering transaction costs and the mental effort of shopping.
2. **Global reach**: no geographic barriers to who can buy from whom.
3. **Universal standards**: shared internet standards cut entry and search costs and make price discovery easier.
4. **Information richness**: the complexity and content of messages enable detailed product information. (Extra context: video, specifications and reviews are typical examples.)
5. **Interactivity**: real-time two-way communication between firm and customer.
6. **Information density**: more and better information for everyone, giving price transparency and less information asymmetry (the seller no longer knows much more than the buyer).
7. **Personalisation and customisation**: messages and products adapt to the individual's behaviour.
8. **Social technology**: user-created content, and network effects that spread distribution.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** A useful way to remember them is by who benefits. Ubiquity, reach and standards widen the market. Richness, interactivity and density improve the information. Personalisation and social technology make it individual and communal.

## The storefront is only the visible edge

The central picture of the session is the storefront as the visible edge. (Extra context: an iceberg is a handy metaphor, the slide does not use it.) The visible part is what the customer sees: search, product, price, order, payment, delivery, service. Beneath it sits **strategy and business model**: what value is offered, to whom, and how the firm captures it (customer, value proposition, revenue model, competitive position, capabilities, partners).

That strategy is executed by five systems, which recur throughout the course:

- **Enable**: infrastructure and transactions (platforms, payments, security, fulfilment).
- **Reach**: marketing and acquisition (search, social, content, offers).
- **Measure**: data and analytics (behaviour, metrics, attribution, retention intelligence).
- **Design**: experience and service (journey, usability, accessibility, trust, recovery).
- **Govern**: consequences and accountability (privacy, fairness, consumer protection, responsibility).

The lesson in one line: **the storefront displays the promise; strategy defines it, and the organisation delivers it.** (Extra context: the later modules map roughly onto these five: infrastructure and payments to Enable, marketing to Reach, analytics to Measure, UX to Design, ethics and privacy to Govern. This is my inference, not stated in the session.)

## Customer simplicity depends on organisational complexity

The slides make a pointed claim: the simpler something looks to the customer, the more complex it usually is inside. Each familiar sign hides work:

- Seeing "in stock" requires accurate inventory and real-time synchronisation.
- A promised delivery time requires capacity, routing and operational commitments.
- A review score requires aggregation, moderation and anti-fraud controls.
- A refund requires policy, exception handling, cash flow and accountability.

## The "Buy now" test

The slide asks what must become true when a customer taps "Buy now": the price is correct, stock exists, the delivery promise is feasible, payment is authorised, fraud risk is acceptable, the order reaches the right fulfilment point, the customer receives confirmation, and failures can be recovered. It then asks: **which item is visible, and which are assumptions?** Only the confirmation really shows on screen; the rest are assumptions the firm has to make true.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** If the warehouse count was wrong, a customer sees "ordered" and later gets a cancellation. The failure is invisible until it hurts.

## Putting it together

The session builds one argument in three steps. First, define e-commerce narrowly by the order boundary so cases can be classified consistently. Second, separate the transaction (e-commerce) from the system that runs the business (e-business). Third, recognise that the visible storefront is the tip of a system: strategy sits underneath, and five systems (Enable, Reach, Measure, Design, Govern) deliver it. The remaining sessions zoom into these systems one by one.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Define e-commerce and distinguish it from e-business.** Give the order-placed-through-a-network definition, note that payment and delivery may be offline, then contrast transaction with wider organisational system, using inventory forecasting versus an app grocery order.
2. **Classify a given boundary case (for example, a smart fridge that reorders) and state what would change your answer.** Apply the single order test, point out that digital discovery or payment is not digital ordering, give your judgement with the test, and name one missing fact. (The slides give no key; marks go for reasoning.)
3. **List and explain the eight characteristics of e-commerce.** Name each with a one-line meaning and an example; group them if helpful.
4. **What is meant by "the storefront is only the visible edge"?** Describe strategy and business model beneath the interface and the five systems (Enable, Reach, Measure, Design, Govern); conclude that the storefront displays the promise while the organisation delivers it.
5. **Trace what must be true behind a "Buy now" click.** List price, stock, delivery, payment, fraud, routing, confirmation and recovery; explain that customer simplicity depends on organisational complexity.
