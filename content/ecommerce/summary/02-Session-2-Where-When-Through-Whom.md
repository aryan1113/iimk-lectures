# Session 2: Where, When and Through Whom Does Commerce Happen?

## Key Concepts

- **Core idea**: commerce is distributed and increasingly mediated; it no longer lives on a website. It can begin in a feed, continue in a message, be executed by an agent and be fulfilled by a network the customer never sees. Key questions: which actor owns the customer relationship, which moment made it e-commerce, and where is the "store"?
- **Three shifts in the visible locus of e-commerce**:
  1. **1995-2006, destination commerce**: customers deliberately visited websites; search, catalogue, cart and checkout sat in one storefront.
  2. **2007-2015, platform commerce**: apps, marketplaces and social platforms distributed discovery while platforms coordinated transactions.
  3. **2016 onward, embedded and agentic commerce**: commerce appears inside content, conversations, super-apps, connected devices and AI-mediated journeys.
  The storefront has not disappeared; it has **fragmented** across touchpoints and intermediaries.
- **Visibility changes along three dimensions**: *Where* (website, marketplace, social feed, message, voice, connected device, AI assistant); *Who* (merchant, platform, creator, aggregator, algorithm, delegated agent); *When* (discovery, evaluation, ordering, payment, fulfilment, service and repeat purchase increasingly overlap).
- **One intent, many interfaces**: a single need spans marketplace (shared discovery and transaction layer), messaging (advice, assisted ordering), AI assistant (delegated discovery or execution), website (owned storefront), social feed (content, creator influence) and super-app (many services in one place). Each actor captures different data and carries different responsibilities.
- **Three forms that blur the boundary**: **social commerce** (discovery, trust and influence arise through social relationships, communities, creators, reviews); **conversational commerce** (search, advice, negotiation, ordering and service through messaging, voice, chatbots or human-assisted conversation); **local/hyperlocal commerce** (digital interfaces coordinate nearby inventory, merchants and fulfilment around the customer's location). Exercise logic: classify a journey by where discovery and trust arise (WhatsApp pharmacy = conversational; Reel to seller site = social; Maps restaurant = local; Blinkit = hyperlocal).
- **India context**: a mobile-first, high-volume, increasingly networked environment (UPI for payments, ONDC as an open network); the national average hides geographic and social differences.
- **Mobile-first changes the whole system, not just screen size**: access becomes personal and continuous (location and context matter); business models respond (hyperlocal supply, subscriptions, creator and assisted commerce, small-ticket transactions); design must handle language, literacy, trust, accessibility and shared devices; expectations become immediate (fast search, simple payment, recovery under interruption and uneven connectivity); operations become real-time (inventory, fraud checks, routing, notifications and support at transaction speed).
- **Quick commerce turns an operational capability into a visible time promise**. Four stages: (1) customer triggers an immediate order (interface cuts friction, stresses urgency, proximity, availability); (2) hyperlocal inventory is selected (nearby fulfilment point, narrow fast-moving assortment); (3) last-mile capacity is committed (picking, packing, rider assignment and updates almost simultaneous); (4) performance data returns (delivery time, substitutions, stock-outs, ratings, complaints shape the next promise and forecast). **Implication: speed is not just a UX claim; it is the customer-facing expression of inventory, labour, logistics, data and risk decisions**, so a firm may sometimes deliberately lengthen its promise to protect trust.
- **Closing lesson**: visibility is distributed, not eliminated. AI agents may conceal discovery, ranking and transaction execution, so as the interface becomes less visible, more must become transparent.

## Why This Matters

Managers cannot hold a customer relationship by owning one storefront; they must decide which touchpoints and intermediaries to join and who controls data and accountability.

## Relevance for Exams / Cases

Describe the three eras and three visibility dimensions, classify a journey as social, conversational or hyperlocal, and explain what a quick-commerce promise demands behind the interface.
