# Guest Session: Four Core Concepts Powering Digital Commerce

## Key Concepts

Class note by Harisankar Radhakrishnan. Examples are mostly Amazon; some are "commonly cited" or illustrative teaching models, and accounts differ on details.

- **1. Scale and the flywheel**. A *flywheel* is a reinforcing loop where each turn makes the next easier. Amazon's: selection and low prices attract customers; more customers attract sellers; more sellers widen selection and volume, cutting cost per order and funding lower prices and service. Customers buy **selection, price and convenience** in any industry; **the slowest link sets the speed**; **operations turn the wheel, technology is the lever**.
  - *Scale* spreads fixed costs over more orders; below a threshold the loop cannot compound. Prime was a bet on early losses for loyalty and frequency. Scale cuts both ways: Amazon failed in China for lack of density.
  - *Open onboarding* (any seller lists without pre-checks) grows selection fast but builds **CX debt** (accumulated cost of poor experiences incurred while growing); complaints are a lagging, sparse signal, bad actors re-register, and poor experiences cut repeat purchase. (A teaching model, not a real policy.)
  - *Gating* restricts who may list certain brands: protects trust and brand but slows selection. Four factors for accepting a brand's request: **platform share** (higher share, less brand leverage); **brand imagery** (trust lost if fakes appear); **distribution** (widely sold goods gain little from gating); **product safety and liability**.
  - **Buy Box**: the featured offer when several sellers sell one product; formula not public, understood to weigh price and convenience.
  - **AI** makes the doing cheaper (catalogue creation, fraud detection, forecasting); choosing stays human (which brands to gate, thresholds, promises). Trap: false positives block legitimate sellers; models miss new tactics.
- **2. Customer experience (quick commerce)**. Expectations keep rising. Quick commerce changes the flywheel: **convenience over selection** (local-store range); **one seller per item** (little seller competition, so who keeps price honest?); **price set by platform, brand-funded offers and market forces**; **a flywheel per city** (density beats national reach).
- **3. Working backwards**. A marketplace serves **primary users (customers), secondary (sellers) and tertiary (advertisers)**; sellers and ads are major revenue sources, so designing only for customers misses much of the product. Designers learn from people around the customer (digital equivalents: sellers, delivery partners, support agents). **Reviews feedback loop**: honest reviews give sellers insight, products improve, better products attract reviews, trust grows, and the review data is hard to copy. **Designing for trust** (Airbnb): host and guest have opposing anxieties; both must be solved or neither joins.
- **4. Data with anecdotes**. A platform sees every search, click and abandoned basket, unlike a store. Heatmaps and **rage clicks** (repeated clicks on something unresponsive) signal frustration; A/B tests treat page elements as hypotheses. *Netflix case lesson*: the "why" ladder showed the purpose was to **reduce the burden of choice**, not just play video. AI shortens the experiment cycle but people choose the metric.
  - **Andon cord** (from lean manufacturing): any customer-service agent can pull a product from the site until root cause is fixed. Lesson: one call can hold more insight than a quarterly report; **data shows how much, anecdotes show why**, and they can disagree. AI reads every call but summaries smooth outliers, so ask for the most unusual complaints, not just themes.

## Why This Matters

The four concepts give a reusable lens: the business's reinforcing loop, its convenience promise, whom it designs for, and whether it hears both data and stories.

## Relevance for Exams / Cases

Draw and critique a flywheel and name its slowest link, decide on gating with the four factors, identify primary/secondary/tertiary users, and explain when to trust an anecdote over a dashboard.
