# Media Planning, Internet (Search) Advertising and OTT

Once you know your goal, audience and message, you have to decide where and when people will actually see it. That is media planning, the "Media" M of the 6M model. This session covers the vocabulary and metrics of traditional media planning (reach, frequency, GRP, CPM), then moves to the internet: search advertising with its auction and click metrics, programmatic buying, and finally connected TV and OTT streaming. The big theme is that measurement changes as you go from TV to digital: from audience-rating metrics to auction, click and conversion metrics.

## Media planning basics

**Media planning** is deciding how to deliver communication to the **target group (TG)**. Four terms to keep distinct:

- **Media class**: a type of medium, such as broadcast, print, out-of-home (OOH), direct or social.
- **Media vehicle**: a specific carrier within a class, such as Zee or Star within TV.
- **Media mix**: using more than one class in the plan. The slides ask why this matters but give no answer.
- **Media schedule**: which days or months the ads run. The **media plan** is the schedule for the chosen mix.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** one plausible answer to why a mix matters is that no single medium reaches everyone and different media do different jobs. The slides leave it open. Similarly, the slide asking what the media vehicle is for newspapers gives no answer (a plausible reading is a specific paper, by analogy with a TV channel).

## The metrics, one by one

**Reach** is the number of different people (unduplicated) who saw the ad at least once. The slides give reach at +1 as 81% (at least once), at +2 as 74% (at least twice) and at +3 as 68% (and so on). **Frequency** is the average number of times the TG sees it. **GRP (gross rating points)** = reach x frequency; it is "gross" because it counts repeat exposures (duplicated reach). **OTS or impressions** count exposures regardless of whether the viewer is in the target group.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** if an ad reaches 40% of the TG and each reached person sees it 3 times on average, GRP = 40 x 3 = 120. The number can exceed 100 because repeats are counted.

**TRP (television rating point)** is a weighted viewership percentage: full viewers count as 1 and partial viewers as a fraction.

> **In class:** 100 women in Kochi, 10 of whom watch the programme *Big Boss Malayalam*. Its **reach is 10%**. If 5 watch the whole programme and 5 watch half, **TRP = (5 x 1) + (5 x 0.5) = 7.5%**. The slide asks whether any other information is needed; the answer shown is how fully each viewer watched. The lesson: reach counts heads, TRP discounts partial viewers.

**Media agency pitch (slide example).** An agency proposes: set GRP at 2x the leading competitor's, which gives a 40% **share of voice** in the launch month; advertise for 4 weeks; spend on GECs (general entertainment channels), news and movies. Reach at +1 is 81% and **OTS would be 12**. This ties three ideas together: GRP (how much exposure you buy), SOV (your slice of the category's exposure, from the first session) and OTS (opportunities to see per person reached).

**CPM** is cost per thousand, used to judge the cost efficiency of a medium: the cost of reaching 1,000 readers, viewers or users. For **television**: cost of one unit of time / programme rating x 1,000. For a **newspaper**: cost of ad space / circulation x 1,000. The slide asks whether CPC has a similar measure in traditional media and gives no answer (read CPC under search below). The catch is that circulation can exceed the target market, so planners use **target CPM** and consider **readers per copy** (the pass-along rate): one copy may be read by several people.


**Effective frequency** is the minimum (threshold) frequency for ensuring a positive response; the slide gives it as three and attributes it to Herbert Krugman.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** the implication that fewer than three exposures may be wasted spend is my inference from the definition.

**Incremental reach** is the new TG audience you add by using a new channel.

## Media research in India

Brands need audience data to know whether they are spending too much or too little and whether the channels and slots they buy are effective and in line with the ratings. For TV, **BARC** (Broadcast Audience Research Council) is an industry body set up on the recommendations of TRAI and the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting. Its three stakeholders are broadcasters, advertisers and agencies, and it measures viewing in roughly 50,000 homes using audio watermarks picked up by "bar-o-meters". For print, the **Indian Readership Survey** is conducted by **MRUC** (Media Research Users Council, a not-for-profit body of advertisers, ad agencies and media owners) with Hansa Research. It is a single-source survey of media and product ownership and usage aimed at readership information.

## Choosing media

**Outbound** media are firm-initiated (TV, radio, magazines, newspapers, websites, social); **inbound** media are consumer-initiated (SEO, podcasts, blogs, retail placement). Class decisions weigh fit with the creative (for example audio-visual), production lead times and the competitive setting. Slides also name mass versus addressable media and paid, owned and earned media without defining them. Vehicle decisions look at unbiasedness and popularity, and expertness and prestige.

## Scheduling patterns

- **Continuous**: even exposure throughout the period.
- **Flighting**: bursts of advertising with inactive gaps between.
- **Pulsing**: a continuous base with intermittent heavy bursts on top.

The distinction to remember: flighting goes dark between bursts; pulsing never goes fully off.

## From linear TV to connected TV

Viewing is shifting to **connected TV (CTV)**: viewers are young, affluent and experience-seeking, helped by cheap data, smart TVs and diverse content, with **co-viewing** (several people on one screen). CTV offers data-driven planning, precise targeting, real-time activation and advanced measurement.

Households fall into four types:

- **Linear-only**: traditional DTH or cable only.
- **Cord-shavers**: also stream, and downgrade to cheaper DTH or cable.
- **Cord-cutters**: fully on OTT or CTV.
- **Cord-nevers**: never had DTH or cable.

**ACR (automatic content recognition)** lets smart devices recognise what is on screen by matching audio or video to a content database. This enables targeted ads, glass-level measurement and second-screen engagement.

## Programmatic buying

**Programmatic** means automated, real-time buying and selling of ad space. The slides compare two routes:

What it offers: publishers get more monetisation opportunities; advertisers get more efficient reach. Key terms: the **ad exchange** is a neutral marketplace where buying and selling happen; **publishers** own websites or apps offering ad space; **advertisers** are brands that want to reach their TG.

- **Ad network**: an aggregator or wholesaler that buys inventory in bulk from publishers, marks it up and resells in bundles. Simple and good for reaching specific verticals, but less transparent (placement and price) with less real-time control. Examples: Google AdSense, InMobi.
- **Ad exchange**: an open, auction-based marketplace where advertisers and publishers connect directly by RTB (real-time bidding), like a stock exchange. Transparent (bid data and site info) and efficient, but complex to manage without DSP/SSP technology. Examples: Google AdX, OpenX, Xandr.

The tools are the **SSP** (supply-side platform, for publishers: manages, prices and sells inventory across exchanges and networks to maximise revenue by exposing it to the most bidders; examples Google Ad Manager, Magnite) and the **DSP** (demand-side platform, for advertisers or their agencies: buys inventory across exchanges in real time using demographics, interests and browsing behaviour, and automates bidding so brands pay only for impressions likely to reach their TG; examples MediaMath, Amazon DSP). Easy way to remember: S for sellers, D for demand/buyers.

## Search advertising (SEM)

A search results page shows **organic** links and **paid** links. Advertisers buy keywords; the search engine earns a fee per click. Match types: exact or standard match is less expensive than broad or advanced match. (That broad match reaches more queries but less precisely is my gloss.) Under "Search ads: what do they offer the brand?" the slide lists only **digital visibility**. On the **search versus display** slide, intent is what search targets: it captures customers right when they are expressing interest, so a higher ROI is possible, but it is most likely to capture only **existing demand**, meaning it finds people already looking rather than creating interest.

Which ads show, and in what order, is decided by the **ad auction** using the **bid amount** (maximum paid per click) and the **quality score** (relevance of keywords and landing page, plus ad extensions). Advertisers choose bid strategies aiming for positions 1-3 versus 4-6. The slide lists "golden triangle: eye-tracking" and, under Google, "preferred ad placement: click-through rate, bounce rate", without more detail. (That the golden triangle is where users' eyes fall is my reading.)

Two kinds of copy matter. **Search-side ad copy** is the ad on the results page. The slide dissects a 1-800-FLOWERS ad into its **headline** ("Official Florist of Mother's Day"), the **display URL** and the **ad copy** line beneath (fresh flower guarantee, delivery to many countries, bouquets and gifts). **Website ad copy** is the landing page. The slides show a hotel search near Kochi where sponsored results from Booking.com, MakeMyTrip and Goibibo are the search-side copy, and the MakeMyTrip and Goibibo property-listing pages they lead to are the website copy.

### Search metrics

- **CPC** = ad spend / clicks.
- **CTR** = clicks / impressions. It measures how effective the search ad copy is. The slide asks whether higher is always better and gives no answer.
- **TCR** = sales / clicks. It measures how well the website copy and design convert visitors.
- **Net revenue** = revenue minus cost. **ROAS** = revenue from ads / ad spend. **Average revenue per booking** = revenue / bookings. **Booking probability** = CTR x TCR.
- **CPC bid** is the highest you would pay; **realised CPC** is what you actually pay, depending on the next-highest bid and quality score.

The slides say: if CTR is low, work on the search ad copy; if TCR is low, work on the website ad copy.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** my reasoning: low CTR means many see the ad but do not click; low TCR means many click but do not buy. The slide also asks whether a higher CTR is always better, with no answer.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** 10,000 impressions, 200 clicks, 10 sales, Rs 2,000 spend: CTR = 2%, TCR = 5%, booking probability = 0.1%, CPC = Rs 10. If each sale earns Rs 400, revenue is Rs 4,000 and ROAS = 2.

## OTT

**OTT** is shown against linear TV through MVPDs (multichannel video programming distributors) and over-the-air broadcast (for example Doordarshan). OTT is **device-independent** (smart TVs from Samsung, LG and Sony, external devices such as Fire TV Stick and Chromecast, or laptops, smartphones and tablets) and **platform-flexible**. Models:

- **Ad-supported**: vMVPD/VSP (bundled live channels over the internet), **AVOD** (free, ad-supported), network apps, and social or video platforms.
- **Non-ad**: **SVOD** (subscription) and **TVOD** (rent or buy per title). AVOD/SVOD hybrids are common as platforms look for multiple revenue streams.

No model is unique; the right one varies with affordability, ad tolerance and viewing preference. **OTT advertising** offers reach, targeting, personalisation, interactivity, measurement and cross-device engagement. Compared with TV it is targeted, personalised, interactive, measurable and data-driven. Profit challenges: **ad-skipping, binge watching and cross-device viewing**.

### OTT metrics

Platforms hold log-ins, viewing history, watch duration, interaction patterns and device usage, so evaluation can be performance-based (completed views, QR scans, purchases) and engagement-based. Basic: **CPM**; **CPV** (cost each time a video ad starts); **CPCV** (cost only for ads played to completion); **vCPM** (viewable CPM: at least 50% of the ad on screen for 1+ second). Attention: **CPH** (cost per hour) and **CPS** (cost per second), where quality of attention counts more than quantity. Engagement: **CPE** (per engagement) and **CPI** (per interaction such as a click, tap, swipe, like, share, comment or QR scan).

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** under CPV an ad is charged each time it starts, even if the viewer skips it; under CPCV only when it plays to the end. Under vCPM an ad that loads off screen and is never seen is not billed as viewable.

## Putting it together

Media planning turns the message into a scheduled, bought and measured plan. Classic metrics (reach, frequency, GRP, TRP, CPM) describe exposure; digital metrics (CPC, CTR, TCR, ROAS) follow the click and sale; OTT adds completion and attention metrics.
