# Message and Creative Strategy

This session covers the "Message" M of the 6M model: how a strategy becomes a story that people notice, remember and connect to the right brand. It moves from the kinds of appeals an ad can make, to the creative brief (the document that hands strategy to the creative team), to why creativity and distinctive brand assets pay off, to a framework for choosing a message type (the FCB grid), and finally to choosing an agency. The through-line is that the brief fixes who, what and why before the creatives decide how.

## What the message is

The **message** is the translation of strategy into stories, answering the consumer's question "why should I use or buy this?". **Ad copy** has two parts: the **appeal** (informational or rational, versus transformational or emotional) and the **execution format** that carries it.

### Product-oriented and consumer-oriented appeals

**Product-oriented appeals** focus on the product's features and leave the benefit implied. The slide lists feature, price, news, popularity and generic appeals. **Consumer-oriented appeals** focus on the benefit to the person. Rational versions are:

- **Self-enhancement**: the consumer reaches their goals by using the product.
- **Service**: ways the product can be used (for example recipes).
- **Savings-through-use**: the saving gained from using it.

Emotional versions use positive emotion (joy, humour, surprise) or negative emotion.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** the slide for this topic is heading-only, so there is no worked example. A recipe on a masala pack is a service appeal in everyday life; a price-led banner is a product-oriented price appeal.

## The creative brief

The **creative brief** is the key document that converts strategy (vision, insight, objectives) into action for the creative team. Think of it as a blueprint answering who, what, where, when and why. A weak brief produces weak creative everywhere: ads, store design, direct messages, websites, social media, logos and brochures. A brief can be **strategic** (multi-year) or **tactical** (one ad), and it should be clear, well crafted, carefully considered and not vague or meaningless. The slides also mention **strategists**: the client's brand manager, or the agency's account planners (consumer research, data insights, brand strategy).

**Who writes it?** It depends on the client's preference: brand manager, agency, or a collaboration built by asking the right questions. The slide favours a **co-created document with mutual ownership**, with inputs from both sides and joint responsibility. The slide says an assignment brief becomes the creative brief, with inputs from both sides and joint responsibility. It also notes a P&G legacy of sending employees to work at agencies before they become brand managers. (That mutual ownership stops either side blaming the other is my reading.)

**When?** After brand essence (the desired brand image) and marketing strategy (STP, marketing objectives and communication objectives). The flow is: corporate strategy, then brand essence and marketing strategy, then marketing objective, then communication objective, then the **creative brief**, then the creatives.

### Elements of the brief

The template reads almost like a sentence:

- **Business opportunity or problem**, and **how brand communication helps** address it.
- **Get**: the target and the insight about them.
- **Who**: what they think, feel and do now.
- **To**: what we want them to think, feel and do, stated so it can be measured.
- **By telling them**: the single-minded proposition, **because**: the reasons to believe.
- **Brand tonality**: the brand's personality in three words.
- **Mandatories**: the non-negotiables.
- **Deliverables**: the assets required.

> **In class (Dove haircare brief):** the slide fills in the template for the launch of Dove's shampoo and conditioner range. *Problem:* a category awash in cliches about shiny, long, thick black hair. *Role of communication:* a launch campaign that informs and starts conversations around the brand. *Get:* free-spirited, real-world women, with the insight that beauty standards are set by someone else when the truth is that every woman is beautiful in her own way. *Who:* currently think all shampoos make the same artificial promises and do not understand her everyday hair problems. *To:* realise Dove celebrates each woman as she is, by nurturing her damaged hair; **measure: market share in one year**. *By telling them:* "you are beautiful already; you only need a little help maintaining it", *because* of a special formulation with conditioners for zero-damage hair. *Tonality:* "elder sister sharing her experiences". *Mandatories:* real, relatable women, minimal make-up, one-quarter moisturising milk, white colour coding. *Deliverables:* a deeply intense, personal conversation across TV and outdoor (phase 1), print (2), digital (3) and activation (4). The lesson: a good brief is specific about the target's insight, the change in thinking wanted, a measurable result and the assets to be produced.

## Communication, message and creative strategy

Three layers of strategy are easy to mix up:

- **Communication strategy**: who, why, what. Who are we talking to, what is the objective, and what should they think, feel or do?
- **Message strategy**: what exactly do we say. The core proposition, the benefit, the takeaway.
- **Creative strategy**: how do we say it. The big idea, the appeal, the tone and the executional approach.

The **brief is the bridge** between communication strategy and the actual advertising idea. Put simply, message strategy says what; creativity decides how to make it compelling. A good creative strategy gives the brand a meaningful role in the consumer's life.

## Why creativity matters

A chart cited on the slides (Accelero, 2023) shows creative quality acting as roughly a 12x multiplier on ROI and brand size around 20x, ahead of levers such as targeting, phasing or media mix. The takeaway is that polishing the media plan helps far less than having strong creative and a strong brand.

Successful advertising is described as **emotion, fluency and time** (with creative quality as the ×12 multiplier). A further slide, titled "Fluidity" (the handout's own wording), says none of this matters without fluency, distinctiveness, codification and branding, which together are **distinctive brand assets**.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** the slides do not define fluency; my reading is that it means people quickly and easily recognise whose ad it is.

### Distinctive brand assets (codes)

A brand builds recognition through **codes**: logo, celebrity, colours, slogan, product shape, jingle, mascot and sonic device. Fluency rises with the number of codes in use, peaking at seven (the "magic number"). Codes should be **distinct, repetitive and continuous**, and the slide notes "Ad^7". A chart attributed to the Effie awards shows the chance of high fluency (audience brand recall) rising as the number of brand codes in the ad grows, with the biggest jump at seven.

Two warnings. First, **market orientation**: you are not the consumer. Brands overestimate how famous their assets are and underestimate how unique they are. Second, the bottom line: **make fewer ads and carry them longer**, so the codes get repeated enough to stick.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** a brand whose jingle plays across every ad for years strengthens the sound-brand link with each repeat, the opposite of changing the campaign every season.

**Measuring emotion** goes beyond conventional research: use eye tracking, EEG, facial coding or biometrics, and GSR (galvanic skin response, a measure of skin conductance).

## The FCB grid

The **FCB grid** (Foote, Cone and Belding; Vaughn, 1980) helps choose the message by two questions: is involvement **high or low**, and is the purchase driven by **thinking or feeling**? It gives four cells, each with a different order of learn, feel and do:

- **High involvement, thinking: informative.** Order: learn, feel, do. Consumers study facts before deciding.
- **High involvement, feeling: affective.** Order: feel, learn, do. Emotion leads, knowledge follows.
- **Low involvement, thinking: habitual.** Order: do, learn, feel. People buy out of habit and learn afterwards.
- **Low involvement, feeling: satisfaction.** Order: do, feel, learn. A quick purchase that is its own reward.

The use is practical: place the product on the grid, then pick the message accordingly. A detailed facts-led ad suits the informative cell; an emotional appeal suits the affective one.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** the grid is on the slide with only the cell labels and learn/feel/do orders. The prose descriptions of each cell and any product placement (for instance a car as high involvement, a snack as low) are my reading, not slide content.

## Choosing and working with agencies

Three types: **full-service** (a wide spectrum of end-to-end IMC; examples Ogilvy, McCann), **boutique** (small size and clients, specialised services; examples Chlorophyll, Taproot, Oxygen) and **in-house** (owned by the client; examples Mudra for Reliance, Lintas for Lever (Lever International Advertising Services), Cheil for Samsung Electronics).

**Hiring process.** Have three or four agencies pitch, but who? Watch TV, YouTube, social media and print for three days and list the ads you envy or like; find out which agencies are behind those ads and shortlist them; meet the agency head and the creative director; check chemistry and have them present their best work. The evaluation parameters: **City** (same city), **Competitors** (eliminate those serving your rivals), **Caliber**, **Chemistry**, **Pedigree**, **Ideas** for the brand, **Interest** (their hunger for your account), **Past work** and **Reference check**.

Two cautions from the slides. Do **not** judge an agency by how much it knows about your business, since that can be learned. And insist on meeting the people who will actually handle the account, because better talent means better work and brand success.

## Putting it together

Strategy is turned into a message in three steps: communication strategy (who, why, what), message strategy (what we say) and creative strategy (how). The creative brief sits between them and works best when co-created. Creativity then pays off when it is emotional, easy to recognise and repeated using consistent distinctive assets. The FCB grid helps choose the type of message, and a carefully chosen agency delivers it.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Draft or critique a creative brief.** Walk through Get, Who, To, By telling them because, tonality, mandatories, deliverables; point out vagueness or missing measurability.
2. **Distinguish communication, message and creative strategy.** Who/why/what; what we say; how we say it; the brief as bridge.
3. **Place a product on the FCB grid and choose the message.** Name the cell and its learn/feel/do order (from the slide). The matching appeal is your own reasoned judgment, which the slides do not supply.
4. **Why does creativity matter, and how do distinctive assets help?** Creative quality as an ROI multiplier; codes build fluency, best repeated and continuous; fewer ads, longer.
5. **How should a firm pick an agency?** Pitch process, the evaluation parameters, avoid judging by business knowledge, meet the actual team.
