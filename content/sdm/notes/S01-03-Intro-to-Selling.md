# Sessions 1-3: Introduction to Selling

These first sessions set the tone for the whole course. Before any technique is taught, the instructor asks what selling is, who needs it and why. The sessions then cover the course map, how business orientation has evolved toward experience, how to re-imagine a customer experience, myths about selling, and the hard-sell versus soft-sell distinction. The overall message is that selling is a customer-centred activity, not a talking contest.

## The course map

The course (Sales and Distribution Management) has three modules. Module 1 is **Selling**: introduction, myths, what makes a good salesperson, hard vs soft selling, empathy, customer heterogeneity, SPIN and its critique, then selling and negotiation, networks and persuasion. Module 2 is **Salesforce management**: selection, motivation, leadership, incentives, evaluation and latest trends. Module 3 is **Distribution management**: channel design, channel management and conflict management. So the course moves from the individual seller, to the team of sellers, to the route to market.

## Why selling matters: the business model view

The instructor opens with "What is selling? Who needs it? Why do you need it?" and then "What is a business model?" The slide splits a business model into **economic logic** and **value creation** and asks "which side is more important?" The slides do not give an answer to that question.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** One way to read the two halves is that economic logic is how the business earns, and value creation is what the customer gets. Selling sits where the two meet. The slides leave the "which is more important" question open.

Two claims are stated outright: **selling is used everywhere** (it is required for any profession) and **great leaders are often great salespeople**.

## The evolution of business orientation

The slides trace a sequence: **production orientation, selling orientation, product orientation, market (customer) orientation**, and then ask "What's next?" and "What more do we want as customers?" The answer shown is the **experience and engagement economy**, i.e. **experience orientation**. The slides show the customer's journey in three stages: **pre-purchase** (research), **purchase** and **post-purchase**.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The slides give only the names of the orientations. A plain-English gloss: production = make things efficiently; selling = push what you have made; product = make the best product; market = start from what customers want; experience = shape the whole journey around the customer.

> **In class:** The slides ask "What could have been better?" next to a pre-purchase / purchase / post-purchase table (repeated several times). They give no answers.

## Market dynamics: VUCA

The slides list four pressures: **volatility, uncertainty, complexity and ambiguity**. They show these leading to a transformation in organisation culture: **agility, ambidexterity, disruption, lean thinking, open-mindedness and an ecosystem mindset**. At the product level the same pressures are linked to **user-driven, dynamic product development, SaaS, experience orientation and omnichannel**.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Plain-English glosses: volatility = things change fast; uncertainty = the future is hard to predict; complexity = many interacting factors; ambiguity = unclear cause and effect. Ambidextrous = running today's business while exploring new things. Ecosystem mindset = seeing yourself as part of a network of partners. SaaS = software sold as a continually updated service. Omnichannel = a seamless experience across channels.

## Re-imagining the customer experience

> **In class:** The slides give a structured exercise in three parts.
> **Part 1, identify a persona:** a name and image, key attributes (adjectives) and a short description, plus **needs** (reasons for taking this journey), **challenges** (pain points or potential frustrations) and **opportunities** (ways your product or service can address the pain points). An **empathy map** is also part of the template.
> **Part 2, map the customer journey:** add the persona, then each journey phase (example: "Onboarding"), with **user activities**, **touchpoints** (interaction points), **thoughts** and a **sentiment/mood meter** from frustrated through neutral to delighted.
> **Part 3:** take only one touchpoint and re-imagine the product, service or customer interaction.
> The slides state no lesson for the exercise.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Empathy maps are commonly drawn around what a person thinks, feels, says and does. A sensible way to use the exercise is to find where the mood dips and fix a concrete touchpoint there.

## Myths about selling

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The slides only list the myths. The brief comments below are our own readings, not the instructor's rebuttals.

The listed myths: **salespeople are born, not made**; **good salespeople are good talkers**; **a good salesperson can sell anything**; **a good salesperson can sell to anyone**; **selling is fun and games and travel** (the slide also names the "**locker room syndrome**"); **get rich quick**; **people don't want to buy**. Later sessions suggest reasons to doubt several of these. For instance, the course stresses questioning and listening over talking, so "good talkers" sits uneasily with it.

## Why people choose sales, and career paths

The slides list broad expectations for choosing selling: **high earnings, quick recognition, freedom and mobility**. Four **career paths**: **career salesperson, sales management path, general management path, entrepreneurial path**.

## What makes a good salesperson

Two essentials are named: **empathy** and **need to conquer**. The slide also asks "Can we predict a good salesperson?" and leaves it open.

> **In class:** The "**Sell me a pen**" exercise sits under "Let's get started on selling". The slides do not say how it went. The slide after, in Sessions 4-6, revisits it with "Investigate before you show your capabilities" as the learning.

## Hard sell versus soft sell

| | Hard sell | Soft sell |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation | Concern for self | Concern for the customer |
| Main activity | Talking | Questions for discussion; listening |
| Offering | Pushing product; presenting features | Providing buying opportunities; presenting benefits |
| Attitude to needs | Advocating without acknowledging | Acknowledging needs |

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Plain-English gloss: a **feature** is a fact about the product; a **benefit** is what it does for the customer. The slides do not say which approach to use; given the course's customer-centred emphasis, soft sell is the one it clearly favours.

## Before you start selling

The session ends with two questions that lead into the next block: "Who is your customer?" and "How do you segment your customers?"

## Putting it together

Business thinking has moved from making and pushing toward understanding and experience. The environment (VUCA) rewards agility. Personas and journey maps are tools for understanding customers; soft selling, empathy and drive describe the seller.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Trace the evolution of business orientation and classify a firm.** List production, selling, product, market, experience; match the firm's behaviour; explain why experience (pre-, during, post-purchase) is where the slides end up.
2. **Contrast hard and soft selling.** Use the table: self vs customer, talking vs listening, features vs benefits, advocating vs acknowledging needs.
3. **Critique a customer journey and propose a redesign.** Persona (needs, challenges, opportunities), journey phases with touchpoints and sentiment, then re-imagine one touchpoint.
4. **Discuss two myths about selling.** Pick two from the list and argue against them using the course's emphasis on empathy and listening (our argument, outside the slides).
5. **What does VUCA mean for organisations?** Name the four pressures and the cultural and product-level responses the slides list.
