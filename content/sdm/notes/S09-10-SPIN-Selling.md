# Sessions 9-10: B2B Selling and SPIN

This is the heart of Module 1. The deck moves from selling in general to B2B selling and introduces SPIN, an investigative method in which carefully ordered questions bring the buyer to state their own needs and the benefits. It also covers what to do after investigating (demonstrating capability), how to open and close a call, and how to improve. The ServiceBox case closes the deck.

## B2B frameworks and the stages of a call

The deck repeats the steps in selling and the question "How does B2B selling differ from B2C?" (no answer on the slides). The **B2B selling frameworks** listed are **investigative selling (SPIN), challenger salesman, value selling and consultative selling**. Only SPIN is developed here; the other three are names only, with no detail.

A call has **four stages**: **opening/preliminaries**, **investigating**, **demonstrating capability** (offering your solutions and capabilities to the customer) and **obtaining commitment**.

## Investigation and why questions work

The slides give four reasons: questions **get the buyer talking**; they **control attention** ("giving vs seeking behavior"); **questions persuade, reasons don't**; and **questions uncover needs**. Questions are **open-ended** or **close-ended**. The class prompt: "What are the questions you will ask while selling a pen?"

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Plain-English gloss: open questions invite a full answer ("how do you use it?"), closed ones a yes/no or short fact ("do you write daily?"). A possible reading of "questions persuade, reasons don't" is that people trust conclusions they reach themselves; the slide does not explain it.

## Implied and explicit needs

The slides say **implied needs are more determinant in small sales, and explicit needs are greater predictors in larger sales.** In the SPIN figure, problem questions lead the buyer to reveal **implied needs**, and need-payoff questions lead the buyer to state **explicit needs**.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The slides do not define the two terms. A plain-English reading: an implied need is a problem or dissatisfaction the buyer mentions ("these machines are rather hard to use"); an explicit need is a clear statement of a want or intention to act. The Contortomat sales call on the slides labels the buyer's "rather hard, but we've learned to get them working" as an implied need.

## The SPIN model

The figure: the seller uses **Situation** questions to establish context, leading to **Problem** questions, so the buyer reveals **implied needs**. These are developed by **Implication** questions, which make the buyer feel the problem more clearly and acutely. **Need-payoff** questions then lead the buyer to state **explicit needs** and **benefits**, which are "strongly related to sales success". (SPIN is credited to Rackham in the compact summary; the slide shows the SPIN(R) mark only.)

### Situation questions

They collect background on the **person** (position, whether they make purchasing decisions, objectives), the **business** (type, growth, sales volume, employees, competitors) and the **operations** (equipment, how long, leased or purchased, who uses it, software). Research on the slides: **not positively related to success; successful calls have fewer of them; inexperienced people ask more; buyers become bored with too many; they must be used carefully.** How to ask: **limit the number**, and phrase them so you sound like **a problem solver rather than a prosecutor**, by linking questions to **buyer statements, personal observations or third-party situations**.

### Problem questions

They **probe problems, difficulties or dissatisfactions** ("Are you satisfied with your present supplier?"). Research: experienced people ask a higher proportion; they are **more strongly linked to success than situation questions**; **in small sales the link is very strong; in large sales, no evidence.** The slide example is a **Micropump** sold to a research laboratory, where each feature maps to a problem: exact chamber capacity (hard to measure additives precisely), titanium construction (corrosive liquids damage steel pumps), silent operation (noise disturbs technicians dictating readings) and modular construction (cleaning takes up to half an hour vs under a minute).

> **In class:** Exercises posed: list **at least five problems** one of your own products solves, looking for **less obvious problems because "they are often the ones that will make or break sales"** (the slide's wording); do the same for a Rolls-Royce, a video game and a tie with Rajnikanth's picture; and, as a travel agency selling discount packages to a frequently travelling consulting firm, say what situation information you need, how much you could find by research, which situation questions you would ask and which problem questions you would follow with. The slides give no answers.

### Implication questions

They ask **what the problem means to the business.** The slides say they are **strongly linked to success in larger sales**, **build up the perception of value**, are **harder to ask than situation or problem questions**, are **very effective when selling to decision makers**, are **powerful in high-technology sales because of high risk perception**, and use **Socrates's method** (the slide notes a "strength and danger" in it without elaborating).

**The Contortomat call.** The seller asks if the division uses Contortomat machines (situation: "yes, three"), whether they are hard to operate (problem: implied need, "rather hard, but we've learned how to get them working"), then offers the Easiflo system. The buyer asks the price (about $120,000) and is amazed: "Just to make a machine easier to use? You must be kidding." The slide labels this **"Cost outweighs value"**, with a diagram of the **seriousness of the problem** against the **cost of the solution**, and the goal that the seriousness of the problem outweighs the cost of the solution.

**Planning implication questions.** The slide gives three steps: write down a likely problem; ask whether it might lead to related difficulties; note the difficulties and the questions they suggest. In the example, a hard-to-use machine may mean output quality problems (ask about scrap levels), operator dissatisfaction (ask about turnover), expensive training (ask about training costs) and too few qualified operators (ask about overtime and recruitment). Sample implication questions: "What effect does that have on output?", "Could that lead to increased costs?", "Will this slow down your proposed expansion?", "Would your customers be upset with this change?"

The slide also quotes Benjamin Franklin's "for want of a nail" verse (shoe, horse, rider, battle, kingdom).

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The verse is shown without commentary. A natural reading is that a small problem can cascade into a large consequence, which is the logic of implication questions. Likewise, the "danger" of the Socratic method is not stated; one possibility is that a run of pointed questions can feel leading. Both are our interpretation.

> **In class:** Exercise: "What are the implications for the problems you have identified for the chosen product?"

### Need-payoff questions

They are **solution-centred**: "Is it important for you to solve this problem?", "Why do you find this solution useful?", "Is there any other way this could help you?" The slide's **psychology**: they make the customer's attention focus on the solution rather than the problem, create a positive problem-solving atmosphere, and **make the customer tell YOU the benefits**. Generic examples: "Why is that important?", "How would that help?", "Would it be useful if...?"

> **In class:** The slide asks "Which is a better question?" comparing "if I could help you save time on this operation, would that be useful to you?" with "...what would that enable you to do that you can't do now?" It does not give the answer, and a further exercise asks which need-payoff questions you would ask for your chosen product.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The second form asks the buyer to state the value in their own words, which fits the slide's point that need-payoff questions make the customer tell you the benefits. We read that as the intended answer, but the slides leave it open.

## Demonstrating capability

The slides split the behaviours:

| Behaviour | Definition | Small sales | Large sales |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Features** | Facts, data, product characteristics | Slightly positive | Neutral or slightly negative |
| **Advantages (Type A benefits)** | Show how products, services or features can be used or can help the customer | Positive | Slightly positive |
| **Benefits (Type B)** | Show how products or services meet **explicit needs** expressed by the customer | Very positive | Very positive |

A benefit **must have a cost saving for the buyer, appeal to the buyer's ego needs, be something you can offer which competitors can't, and gives a buying motive.** Research: **don't demonstrate capabilities too early, beware of advantages/Type A benefits, be careful with new products.**

## Opening and closing

**Opening.** Smaller sales: relate to the buyer's personal interests and make an **opening benefit statement**. Larger sales: **who you are, why you're there, your right to ask questions.** Research: first impressions show some evidence in small sales but much less in large ones, and **successful people use a wide variety of openings.**

**Closing** is tactics used to induce purchase or acceptance. Successful sellers **close on their fifth attempt.** Expert advice: closing techniques are strongly related to success; use many types; close frequently. **Research on traditional closes: they do not work with larger sales, with sophisticated buyers, or where your objective is an ongoing relationship; not closing and over-closing are equally bad.** For a larger sale: **focus on investigating, check key concerns, summarise benefits, propose a commitment.**

## Becoming a good salesperson

**Pick just one behaviour, choose safe calls, quantity more than quality, try at least three times before judging.**

## Putting it together

SPIN moves from context to the buyer stating needs and benefits. Sale size matters throughout: problem questions and quick closes are linked to success in small sales, while implication questions, a careful opening and an investigative close matter in large ones.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Write S, P, I and N questions for a product.** A few researched situation questions, problem questions about difficulties, implication questions that follow the consequences, need-payoff questions that ask how a solution would help.
2. **Why do implication questions matter in large sales?** The slides link them to success in larger sales, to perceived value, to decision makers and high-tech sales, and show the Contortomat price shock where cost outweighed the problem's seriousness.
3. **Distinguish features, advantages and benefits.** Use the table, including impact by sale size and what a benefit must do.
4. **How should a large B2B sale be closed?** Traditional closes fail with large sales and sophisticated buyers; investigate, check concerns, summarise benefits, propose a commitment.
5. **Implied vs explicit needs and sale size.** Implied needs matter more in small sales, explicit in large; definitions are a plain-English gloss (outside the slides).
