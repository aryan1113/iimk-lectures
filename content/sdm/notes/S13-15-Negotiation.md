# Sessions 13-15: Negotiation

After learning to sell through questions, the class turns to the moment where the deal is shaped. The deck defines negotiation as the means by which people deal with differences, then builds a toolkit: distributive vs integrative negotiation, four key aspects (value creation, BATNA, reservation price, ZOPA), five styles, communication, and a catalogue of tactics. A live role play was part of the sessions. The stated takeaway is "Value creation, value creation, value creation!"

## What negotiation is

The slides define it as the **means by which people deal with differences** and ask "What are the negotiation situations which you have been in?" They list **many paths to a deal: distributive negotiation, integrative negotiation, and the negotiator's dilemma.**

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** "The negotiator's dilemma" appears as a title only. It is commonly understood as the tension between creating value (growing what is on the table) and claiming value (getting your share of it).

> **In class:** Two opening prompts: "This money is yours. How will you divide it with the person sitting next to you?" and "How will you negotiate a price for this carpet?" The slides give no answers. (Our reading: both involve splitting a fixed sum, which sets up the distributive idea.)

## Distributive negotiation

The slides: parties **compete over the distribution of a fixed sum of value**; "who will claim the maximum value" is the key question; **a gain on one side is made at the expense of the other.** It is a **win-lose** situation, the **value at stake is fixed**, the seller wants as high a price as possible and the buyer as low as possible.

The worked scenario on the slides: **Acme Manufacturing** wants to buy **10,000 switches** from **Best Parts**. Acme's procurement manager wants **$1.75**; Best Parts' sales manager wants **$2 per widget**. The slide asks "Who wins in distributive negotiation?" With a fixed pie, the issue is how the 25-cent gap between $1.75 and $2 gets split, and any cent one side gains is a cent the other loses (our arithmetic on the slide's numbers).

**Keys to distributive negotiation** (summary slide): starting with a strong position, a "take it or leave it" strategy, high/low-balling. The slides also pose "Hard bargainers: what should you do?" and "How will you ask for a price for these books?" with no answers.

## Integrative negotiation

The slides: parties **cooperate to achieve maximum benefits by integrating interests**; the aim is **creating value and claiming it.** The same Acme / Best Parts scenario is shown again, asking "How will it work in integrative negotiation?" The slide gives no answer.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Rather than only arguing over $1.75 vs $2, an integrative approach could trade other things each side values. Quantity, delivery, payment terms or services are our examples of such levers; the slides do not name them for this scenario. Another exercise, selling Pollachi coconuts at Rs 50 a piece, also has no answer on the slides; one reading is that it invites you to go beyond price.

**Distributive vs integrative**: win-lose with fixed value vs win-win with value creation. Choosing between them is not discussed beyond the summary slide.

## Four key aspects of integrative negotiation

### 1. Value creation

It is "key to integrative negotiation", and the prompt is "How will you create/demonstrate value to your customer?" Three phone scenarios are posed: (1) the customer negotiates the price of an **Apple phone**; (2) **Samsung Galaxy M30 Rs 13,999** against **Redmi Note 7 Pro Rs 13,000**; (3) the **M30 at Rs 13,999 at your local store** against the same M30 at **Chroma** at Rs 13,999. The next slide asks "What was the difference in the scenarios?" and shows an arrow from **Scenario 1: "Highlighting values"** to **Scenario 3: "Creating values"**. Scenario 2 is not placed on the arrow.

How to create value (slides): be creative and think on the spot; look for avenues that give you **a gain and a non-loss for the other party**, such as getting them to **buy more quantity**, **payment options**, **value adds**, **free services**, a **back-end discount for quantity sold** (e.g. Ritchie Street), and an **annual maintenance contract** (e.g. maintaining existing defective machines at Rs 2k per machine). The slides give distributor-retailer sales and house negotiations as contexts. The bottom line: **"Find a value. Make a sale. This is the goal."** and "Do it ethically."

> **In class:** "The donkey and the horse": the customer wants 25 blue ink pens, you want to sell 40 sketch pens. "How will you negotiate?" The slides give no answer.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** One reading of this exercise is that the two sides want different things, so there may be a trade that suits both. That is our interpretation.

### 2. BATNA

**Best alternative to a negotiated agreement.** The slide example: a consultant is to meet a client about a month-long assignment; before the meeting, the consultant considers the best alternative, **alternate work worth Rs 4,00,000.** Five ways to improve your position using BATNA: **know your BATNA, improve your BATNA, identify the other party's BATNA, weaken the other party's BATNA, provide a win-win value proposition.**

> **In class:** Scenario: an employee asks for a six-month leave of absence, fully prepared with justifications. "How will you use BATNA to negotiate?" No answer is given.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** Reading the consultant example, the Rs 4 lakh alternative suggests they should not accept a deal worth less. The slide does not spell this out.

### 3. Reservation price

The **walk-away price**, the **least favourable point at which one will accept the deal**, **derived from BATNA but not always the same.** The slide example gives two prices only: **Rs 900 per square foot in Palayam (current house)** versus **Rs 1250 per square foot in Kunnamangalam**, and does not explain how it relates BATNA to reservation price.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** A plausible reading is that the alternative's price anchors your limit, but other factors (location, convenience) can shift it, which would explain why the two need not match.

### 4. ZOPA

The **zone of possible agreement.** The slides give the term and link a video, with no definition text.

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** ZOPA is commonly defined as the overlap between the two sides' reservation prices. For example, if a seller will accept no less than Rs 100 and a buyer will pay up to Rs 120, the zone is Rs 100-120. If the ranges do not overlap, there is no deal.

## Role play

Students negotiated in buying and selling teams. The checklist: **negotiation skills** (emphasising value, thinking for the other party, moving toward integrative negotiation), **preparedness** (awareness of BATNA, ZOPA, reservation or walk-away price), **communication**, and **situation-specific criteria: adaptability and perseverance.**

## Five negotiation styles (Lewicki and Hiam)

| Style | Meaning on the slide | Link |
|---|---|---|
| **Competition** (win-lose) | "I win, you lose"; focus on outcome, not relationship | Distributive |
| **Collaboration** (win-win) | "I win, you win"; high focus on outcome and relationship; requires more effort from both | Integrative |
| **Compromise** (split the difference) | "I win/lose some, you win/lose some" | In between distributive and integrative |
| **Accommodation** (lose-win) | "I lose, you win"; focus on relationship; you agree to distributive negotiation with focus on future relation | Distributive |
| **Avoidance** (lose-lose) | "I lose, you lose"; you don't care about outcome or relation | Neither |

> **Extra context (not on the slides):** The slides do not say when to use which style. One reasonable guide from the table: collaboration when both outcome and relationship matter; accommodation when the future relationship matters more than this deal.

## Communication

**Politeness, humility, pleasing personality, product awareness, speaking soft but with persuasive arguments, always speaking for the customer.** The best negotiations take place when **both parties think about value for each other.** And: **speak the same language when you sell; avoid speaking the same language when you buy/negotiate.** The slides do not explain the last pair.

## Tactics to use or be aware of

- **Foot-in-the-door**: start with a small initial request and then scale up.
- **Door-in-the-face**: start with an unreasonable request, then follow with a more reasonable one.
- **Take it or leave it** (the "brink"): use if you are into hard bargaining.
- **Bogey**: make the other party feel you care about an issue that is important to them, so they feel they should do the same for you (the principle of reciprocity). The AMA slide describes it as pretending a particular issue is important.
- **Make it personal**: personal experience and emotion to connect.
- **Ask questions**: understand the other side's motivation and buy time to think.
- **Behavioural theories**: anchoring, mental accounting, prospect theory, decoy effect, endowment effect.
- **Good cop / bad cop**: in a two-person team one is reasonable and the other is not.
- **Lack of authority**: you or your counterpart cannot make decisions. The slides give no counter.
- **Deadlines**: a deadline that pressures the other party into deciding.
- **High-balling / low-balling**: open with an extremely high or low offer hoping the other party reconsiders its reservation price and goal. **Counter** by calling them out, asking them to justify the price, or using BATNA-based arguments.
- **Nibble**: at the end, ask for a small concession not discussed earlier; the other party may be worn out and consent.
- **Snow job**: overwhelm the other party with information so they are confused about what matters.

**AMA survey on the most common techniques** (slides): high-ball/low-ball 29%, bogey 17%, snow job 12%, nibble 11%, lack of authority 11%, good cop/bad cop 8%, deadlines 6%, the brink 6%.

## Summary and readings

The slides' summary repeats the types, the keys to each, the styles (integrative: collaborating and compromising; distributive: competing and accommodating; plus avoiding), communication and tactics, and ends with "Value creation, value creation, value creation! Use BATNA, ZOPA, reservation prices for effective value creation propositions." Suggested books include *Getting to Yes* and *Never Split the Difference* (a counter-perspective).

## Putting it together

Preparation (BATNA, reservation price, ZOPA) sets your limits; value creation is the route to better deals; style says how much you weigh the relationship; tactics are moves to recognise and answer.

## Likely exam questions

1. **Given two reservation prices, find the ZOPA.** ZOPA is the overlap (the definition is outside the slides); if none, no deal.
2. **Name the tactic in a vignette and a counter.** Identify it from the definitions above. The slides give a counter only for high/low-balling (call it out, ask for justification, use BATNA).
3. **Recommend a style with justification.** Use the style table; the guidance on when each fits is our reasoning, outside the slides.
4. **Distinguish distributive and integrative negotiation; give a value-creation example.** Fixed vs value-creating; levers from the slides (quantity, payment options, value adds, free services, back-end discount, maintenance contract).
5. **How can you improve your BATNA position?** The five slide steps: know, improve, identify theirs, weaken theirs, win-win proposition.
