# Sessions 9-10: B2B Selling and SPIN

## Key Concepts

- **B2B frameworks**: investigative selling (SPIN), challenger salesman, value selling, consultative selling.
- **Four stages of a call**: opening/preliminaries → investigating → demonstrating capability → obtaining commitment.
- **Why questions work**: they get the buyer talking, control attention, uncover needs; questions persuade, reasons don't. Use open and closed questions deliberately.
- **Implied vs explicit needs**: implied needs (problems, difficulties) predict success in small sales; explicit needs (stated wants) predict success in large sales. The seller's job is to turn implied into explicit needs.
- **SPIN model** (Rackham):
  - **Situation**: background facts. Keep few; they bore buyers and are not linked to success; inexperienced sellers over-ask. Do desk research first; sound like a problem-solver, not a prosecutor, by linking questions to buyer statements, observations or third-party situations.
  - **Problem**: probe difficulties and dissatisfactions. More tied to success than situation questions, strongly so in small sales. Exercise: list five problems your product solves, including non-obvious ones.
  - **Implication**: explore consequences of the problem for the business. Hardest to ask, but the most linked to success in large sales, builds perceived value, works on decision makers and in high-tech sales (high perceived risk). Plan them by listing a problem, then related difficulties (e.g. hard-to-use machine → quality problems, overtime, recruitment). Why they exist: if you offer a solution after only situation and problem questions, the price shocks the buyer ("$120,000 just to make a machine easier to use?"). Implication questions raise the seriousness of the problem above the cost of the solution. Strength and danger of this Socratic method: powerful, but can feel leading.
  - **Need-payoff**: solution-centred questions where the buyer states the benefit ("How would that help?"). Creates a positive atmosphere and makes the customer tell you the benefits. Better: "what would that enable you to do that you can't now?" than "would that be useful?", because the buyer states the value.
- **Features, advantages, benefits**: features (facts) are neutral or negative in large sales; advantages/Type A (how a feature helps) mildly positive; benefits/Type B (meets an explicit need) very positive. A benefit must save the buyer cost, appeal to ego needs, be something competitors can't offer, and gives a buying motive. Don't demonstrate capability too early; beware Type A benefits and new products.
- **Opening**: small sales relate to the buyer's interests with a benefit statement; large sales state who you are, why you are there, your right to ask questions. First impressions matter less in large sales, and successful sellers use a wide variety of openings, so no single opening is best.
- **Closing**: expert advice says use many closes and close often (successful sellers close around the fifth attempt). But research shows traditional closes fail in large sales, with sophisticated buyers and where relationships matter; over-closing and not closing are equally bad. For large sales: investigate, check concerns, summarise benefits, propose a commitment.
- **Improving**: pick one behaviour, practise on safe calls, quantity over quality, try at least three times before judging.

## Why This Matters

SPIN is the course's central B2B method; the research findings tell you which question type to emphasise by sale size. The ServiceBox case applies it.

## Relevance for Exams / Cases

Expect to write S-P-I-N questions for a product, explain why implication questions matter in large sales, and distinguish features, advantages and benefits.
